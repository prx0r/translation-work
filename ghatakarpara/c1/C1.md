## ghat-p01 — the lament

## SUMMARY

The separated beloved's lament opens the poem: Kāma arises in the heart to kill, and the beloved who is absent is the one who arises with him. The cruel husband serving a foreign land will kill her without himself; she demands he release his foreign love or face his wife's accusation. The cātaka, thirsty for rain, begs water — the lover thirsts for the absent beloved, and the cloud becomes the messenger. The peacock-flock is sounded by clouds; what delight without her? Thinned by cloud-arrival, tormented by hard Kāma, fallen into sorrow-ocean's water, she is protected only by remembrance of his virtue. The rivers' water melts the impurity; why does he regard her, not wretched? Pained by deep cloud-rasa, she asks when she shall abandon the burning of separation-born sorrow.

## FUNCTION

Foundational passage: establishes the sandeśa-kāvya device (cloud as messenger) and the śṛṅgāra rasa of love-in-separation. The cātaka/cloud figure becomes the genre's signature.

## KEY TERMS

* **pramadā** — beloved: the separated woman, the poem's voice.
* **proṣita** — separated: the husband abroad, the absence that drives the lament.
* **madana** — Kāma: the god of love, the force that arises in the heart.
* **cātaka** — cātaka bird: drinks only rain; figure for the thirsting lover.
* **pathika** — traveler: the suffering lover on the road.
* **ambuda** — cloud: the messenger, the poem's addressee.
* **toyadāgama** — cloud-arrival: the event that thins the beloved, brings the messenger.
* **sādhya** — to be tormented: the beloved's state under Kāma's pressure.

## LOCAL CONTEXT

First of three passages; reader has nothing presupposed. The lament's voice (separated beloved) is the genre's founding stance; the cloud-messenger device is introduced here and reused in p02–p03.

## EXPLANATION

The passage moves in four movements. First, Kāma and absence (1–4): desire arises in the heart alongside the beloved's absence; the cruel foreign-serving husband will kill her without himself; she demands he release his rival love or face accusation. The dilemma is hers to voice, his to resolve. Second, the messenger device (5–6): the cātaka, thirsty for rain, begs water; the suffering traveler is the beloved. The bird becomes the lover's figure — thirsting, appealing to the cloud. Third, the cloud arrives (7–8): the peacock-flock is sounded; what delight without her? Thinned by cloud-arrival, tormented by hard Kāma, fallen into sorrow-ocean. The landscape responds to her mood. Fourth, protection and appeal (9): remembrance of his virtue alone protects her; the rivers' water melts impurity; why does he regard her, not wretched? Pained by deep cloud-rasa, she asks when she shall abandon the burning of separation-born sorrow. The lament closes not in despair but in the sustaining memory.

## BOUNDARY / OPEN

HOW the cloud carries the message is unexplained: the sandeśa device is implicit, not stated; Abhinava's vivṛti makes it explicit. The cātaka's thirst is both literal (bird drinks only rain) and figurative (lover thirsts) — both readings live. *sādya te* (v.8) may be a compression of sādyate; the text is kept as-is. Whether the beloved's accusation is just or rhetorical is unresolved.

## RELATED

* Meghadūta (Kālidāsa) — the genre's most famous successor; same cloud-messenger device.
* Paryantapancasika (Abhinavagupta) — same author's vivṛti practice.
* Stotra 1 of Śivastotrāvalī — same author's devotional register, different rasa.

## ghat-p02 — the beauty of the grove

## SUMMARY

The grove's beauty is for Kāma's sake: the groves of abodes shine today because of him. The beloved is chief of groves by sprouts, eye-festival of youths. The kuṭaja is laughed at by flowers; the poet bows, hard to laugh at you. The nīpa's flower-looked-at body would be released quickly. The bee, observing honey's season, kisses the yūthikā creeper. By waters scattered, dust on earth; sun and moon are not seen. New-cloud-drunk, peacocks cry at cloud-arrival. With Indra's weapon, the cloud resounds, bringing the elephants' ardor, mountain-like. Loud roar, water in caves falls, wonderful-formed beauties.

## FUNCTION

Vibhāva passage: the grove is the determinant of śṛṅgāra, not backdrop. The kuṭaja's mockery and the nīpa's release are the rasa's consequents.

## KEY TERMS

* **mañjarī** — sprout: the beloved identified with the grove's beauty.
* **kuṭaja** — kuṭaja tree: mocked by showy flowers, bowed to by the poet.
* **nīpa** — nīpa tree: whose body is released to the bees.
* **bhramara** — bee: the lover kissing the creeper.
* **yūthikā** — yūthikā creeper: the beloved.
* **rajas** — dust: of passion, obscuring the luminaries.
* **śikhin** — peacock: drunk with the new cloud.
* **jalada** — cloud: roars with Indra's thunderbolt.
* **ibha** — elephant: responds with ardor.

## LOCAL CONTEXT

Middle passage; reader knows the lament (p01). The beauty is the rasa's determinant, not decoration.

## EXPLANATION

The passage moves in five movements. First, Kāma's sake (10–11): the groves shine for Kāma; the beloved is chief of groves by sprouts, eye-festival of youths. Second, the kuṭaja and the nīpa (12–13): the kuṭaja is laughed at by flowers; the poet bows, hard to laugh at you; the nīpa's flower-looked-at body would be released quickly. The mockery is affectionate; the release is surrender. Third, the bee and the creeper (14): having observed honey's season, the bee kisses the yūthikā creeper — the lover's anubhāva. Fourth, dust and peacocks (15–16): by waters scattered, dust on earth; sun and moon are not seen; new-cloud-drunk, peacocks cry. The rasa overwhelms perception. Fifth, cloud and water (17–18): with Indra's weapon, the cloud resounds, bringing the elephants' ardor; loud roar, water in caves falls, wonderful-formed beauties. The close is aesthetic, not narrative.

## BOUNDARY / OPEN

Whether *puṣpanirīkṣitāpadeham* (v.13) means flower-looked-at body or body looked at by flowers — both live. The kuṭaja's mockery is both affectionate and real. The nīpa's release is both surrender and destruction. These remain open; the poem holds them unresolved.

## RELATED

* Meghadūta (Kālidāsa) — successor genre; same cloud-messenger, same landscape rasa.
* Paryantapancasika — same author's vivṛti practice.

## ghat-p03 — the close

## SUMMARY

Clouds thundering make travelers long; sorrow grows in those, endless. They honor the love-festival with beloveds; cloud-arrival, dear friend, they bring. May I be conquered by which poet, by yamakas, other; to him I pour water in ghaṭakarpara. The lament and beauty are framed by the poet's skill; the poem is self-referential, not narrative.

## FUNCTION

Close passage: the rasa shifts from śṛṅgāra to vira/adbhuta. The poem ends not in the lover's voice but in the poet's — the tribute is to skill, not to love alone.

## KEY TERMS

* **utkaṇṭhā** — longing: awakened by the thundering cloud.
* **ratyutsava** — love-festival: the communal anubhāva.
* **priyasakhi** — dear friend: who brings the cloud-arrival.
* **yamaka** — yamaka figure: same word, different meaning; the poet's skill.
* **ghaṭakarpara** — potsherd: the vessel of the offering, the poem's namesake.

## LOCAL CONTEXT

Final passage; reader knows lament (p01) and beauty (p02). The close reframes both as the poet's achievement.

## EXPLANATION

The passage moves in three movements. First, longing and sorrow (19): clouds thundering make travelers long; sorrow grows in those, endless. The cloud is now the messenger; its thunder awakens longing. Second, love-festival (20): they honor the love-festival with beloveds; cloud-arrival, dear friend, they bring. The rasa's anubhāva is communal. Third, tribute (21): may I be conquered by which poet, by yamakas, other; to him I pour water in ghaṭakarpara. The poet who conquers by yamakas is honored; the speaker yields to that skill. The close is in the poet's voice, not the lover's.

## BOUNDARY / OPEN

Which words form the yamaka is unidentified (Abhinava's vivṛti would say). The speaker of v.21 may be the poet himself (self-tribute) or a rival voice — both live. The potsherd (ghaṭakarpara) is both literal vessel and metaphor for the poem itself. These remain open.

## RELATED

* Meghadūta (Kālidāsa) — successor genre; same cloud-messenger.
* Kāvya-prakāśa (Mammaṭa) — yamaka as alaṅkāra; the poem's self-reference.
* Abhinavagupta's vivṛti (KSTS 67) — the commentary that frames the poem.

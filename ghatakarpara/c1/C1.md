## ghat-p01

## SUMMARY

Śṛṅgāra is performed, not described: the separated beloved's voice, her appeal to the cloud, and the landscape's answering her are all determinants (vibhāva) of the rasa. The cloud is never told to carry a message — that is dhvani, and it is what makes this work the genre's first instance. The voice closes not in despair but in sustaining memory: the sthāyibhāva (love) transforms the sorrow (vyabhicāri) into something relishable. Kāma arises in the heart to kill; the beloved who is absent arises with him; the cātaka, thirsty for rain alone, begs water; the peacock-flock sounds at the clouds; she is protected only by remembrance of his virtue.

## FUNCTION

Establishes the sandeśa-kāvya device and śṛṅgāra as the sthāyibhāva. The cātaka/cloud figure becomes the genre's signature; every later sandeśa-kāvya descends from this device.

## KEY TERMS

* **pramadā** — beloved: the separated woman, the voice and its sthāyibhāva.
* **proṣita** — separated: the husband abroad; the absence that drives the voice.
* **madana** — Kāma: the god of love, the force that arises in the heart.
* **cātaka** — cātaka bird: drinks only rain; figure for the thirsting lover.
* **ambuda** — cloud: the messenger, the addressee and vibhāva.

## LOCAL CONTEXT

The separated beloved's voice is the genre's founding stance; the cloud-messenger device is introduced here.

## EXPLANATION

Kāma and absence: desire arises in the heart alongside the beloved's absence; the cruel foreign-serving husband will kill her without himself; she demands he release his rival love or face accusation. The dilemma is hers to voice — the rasa is in the voicing. The messenger device: the cātaka, thirsty for rain alone, begs water; the suffering traveler is the beloved. The bird becomes the lover's figure, appealing to the cloud. This is dhvani: the cloud is never told to carry a message. The cloud arrives: the peacock-flock is sounded; what delight without her? Thinned by cloud-arrival, tormented by hard Kāma, fallen into sorrow-ocean. The landscape responds to her mood — the rasa is not in the woman alone but in the world's answering her. Protection and appeal: remembrance of his virtue alone protects her; the rivers' water melts impurity; why does he regard her, not wretched? Pained by deep cloud-rasa, she asks when she shall abandon the burning. The voice closes not in despair but in sustaining memory — the sthāyibhāva transforms the sorrow into something relishable.

## BOUNDARY / OPEN

HOW the cloud carries the message is unexplained: the sandeśa device is implicit; Abhinava's vivṛti makes it explicit. The cātaka's thirst is both literal and figurative — both readings live. *sādya te* (v.8) may be a compression of sādyate. Whether the accusation is just or rhetorical is unresolved. The messenger's destination is inferred, not declared.

## RELATED

* Meghadūta (Kālidāsa) — successor genre; same cloud-messenger, larger scale.
* Stotra 1 of Śivastotrāvalī — same author's devotional register, different rasa.

## ghat-p02

## SUMMARY
Beauty is not decoration but cause: the groves shine for Kāma's sake, not the reverse. The grove is the vibhāva (determinant) of śṛṅgāra; the rasa overwhelms perception — dust of passion obscures sun and moon, new-cloud-drunk peacocks cry. The kuṭaja's mockery and the nīpa's release are consequents (anubhāva), not ornament: the poet sides with the humble tree over showy flowers. The bee kisses the yūthikā creeper. The rasa is aesthetic: the rasa IS the point.

## FUNCTION

Vibhāva passage: the grove is the determinant of śṛṅgāra, not backdrop. The kuṭaja's mockery and the nīpa's release are consequents (anubhāva), not ornament. What makes beauty beautiful? It serves the rasa.

## KEY TERMS

* **mañjarī** — sprout: the beloved identified with the grove's beauty.
* **kuṭaja** — kuṭaja tree: mocked by showy flowers, bowed to by the poet.
* **nīpa** — nīpa tree: whose body is released to the bees.
* **bhramara** — bee: the lover kissing the creeper.
* **rajas** — dust: of passion, obscuring the luminaries.

## LOCAL CONTEXT

The same cloud now arrives and the landscape answers.

## EXPLANATION

Five movements, each a determinant or consequent. First, Kāma's sake: the groves shine for Kāma; the beloved is chief of groves by sprouts. The grove exists for the rasa — this is the thesis. Second, kuṭaja and nīpa: the kuṭaja mocked by flowers; the poet bows; the nīpa's body released to bees. The mockery is affectionate (the poet sides with the humble tree); the release is surrender. Both are anubhāva. Third, bee and creeper: the bee kisses the yūthikā creeper — the lover's anubhāva in miniature. Fourth, dust and peacocks: dust of passion obscures sun and moon; new-cloud-drunk, peacocks cry. The rasa overwhelms perception — the sthāyibhāva transforms the landscape into its own body. Fifth, cloud and water: the cloud resounds with Indra's weapon; water falls into wonderful caves. The rasa is aesthetic: the rasa IS the point.

## BOUNDARY / OPEN

Whether *puṣpanirīkṣitāpadeham* (v.13) means flower-looked-at body or body looked at by flowers — both live. The kuṭaja's mockery is both affectionate and real. The nīpa's release is both surrender and destruction. These remain open as vyabhicāri that the sthāyibhāva integrates without dissolving.

## RELATED

* Meghadūta (Kālidāsa) — successor genre; same cloud-messenger, same landscape rasa.

## ghat-p03

## SUMMARY

The rasa shifts from śṛṅgāra to vira/adbhuta: love and landscape are framed by the poet's skill, not by love alone. Clouds thundering make travelers long; sorrow grows, endless. Lovers honor the love-festival; cloud-arrival, dear friend, they bring. Then the tribute: may I be conquered by which poet, by yamakas; to him I pour water in ghaṭakarpara. The sthāyibhāva (love) resolves into the poet's own delight (camatkāra) at his craft. The potsherd is both vessel and namesake — the potsherd names itself at its close.

## FUNCTION

The rasa shifts from śṛṅgāra to vira/adbhuta. The tribute is to skill, not to love. The voice ends in the poet's.

## KEY TERMS

* **utkaṇṭhā** — longing: awakened by the thundering cloud.
* **ratyutsava** — love-festival: the communal anubhāva.
* **yamaka** — yamaka figure: same word, different meaning; the poet's skill.
* **ghaṭakarpara** — potsherd: the vessel of the offering, its namesake.

## LOCAL CONTEXT

Both are reframed as the poet's achievement.

## EXPLANATION

Three movements, each a rasa shift. First, longing and sorrow: clouds thundering make travelers long; sorrow grows, endless. The cloud is now the messenger; its thunder awakens longing. The rasa is still śṛṅgāra but the sthāyibhāva is shifting — the lover's longing becomes the poet's. Second, love-festival: they honor the love-festival with beloveds; cloud-arrival, dear friend, they bring. The anubhāva is communal. Third, tribute: may I be conquered by which poet, by yamakas; to him I pour water in ghaṭakarpara. The poet who conquers by yamakas is honored; the speaker yields to that skill. The voice ends in the poet's — self-referential. The śṛṅgāra resolves into vira (heroic skill) and adbhuta (wonder at the yamaka's craft). The potsherd is both vessel and namesake.

## BOUNDARY / OPEN

Which words form the yamaka is unidentified (Abhinava's vivṛti would say). The speaker of v.21 may be the author himself or a rival voice — both live. The potsherd is both literal vessel and metaphor for the vessel. The rasa shift is my reading, flagged as application, not attribution.

## RELATED

* Meghadūta (Kālidāsa) — successor genre; same cloud-messenger.
* Abhinavagupta's vivṛti (KSTS 67).

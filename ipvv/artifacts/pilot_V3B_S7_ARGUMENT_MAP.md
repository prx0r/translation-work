## 1. What is at issue

**The question:** What makes a cognition "true" (satya)? In kārikā 1, Abhinavagupta said the
action-cognitions are true by two things: their *stability* (sthairya) and their *efficacy* (upayoga,
= arthakriyā, the capacity to bring about its intended effect). But "efficacy" is a slippery criterion.
This whole passage is Abhinava testing and defending it against a Buddhist (Vijñānavāda/Sautrāntika-style)
objection about the causal relation between a cognition and its object.

**The Buddhist problem:** Buddhists deny that a real external object is the cause of a cognition — and
they deny causal relations between momentary phenomena anyway. Abhinava wants to say: the *action-
cognition* is true *because* it has causal efficacy (it gets you to the village). The Buddhist counters:
causal efficacy can't be the mark of truth, because (a) the object that would be the cause is
un-graspable, and (b) momentariness makes the whole cause/effect talk incoherent.

## 2. The argument, reconstructed step by step

**Step 0 — where efficacy holds.** [Abhinava, from the prior passage:]
For anything, being *the cause of a given cognition* (tajjñānakāritā) is what establishes that thing's
having causal efficacy (arthakriyākāritva) and hence its being-ness (sattva). But this is *not* the case
for the blue [the object] that is manifest *at the very same time* as the cognition. [i.e. the object
is simultaneous with the cognition, so it can't be its cause — a cause must precede its effect.]

**Objection (nanu):** Fine — let even what is simultaneous with the manifestation be the cause.

**Abhinava's first reply:** Then we'd have to say "two things of equal time stand in the cause-effect
relation" — which is nonsense. But that's not really my point; it's a secondary refutation. My real
point: it is *the being-manifest* itself (bhāsamānatā) that, at that time, is the *ground* (nimitta) of
calling something a cause. And that manifest-ness is no different in the case of the two-moon [an
illusion]. So manifest-ness alone can't be the mark of truth — otherwise the two-moon would be true too.

**Step 1 — the positive criterion (Abhinava):** Consider the blue. That which *accompanies* the
presence and absence [anvaya-vyatireka] of the blue — i.e. is present when the blue is present and
absent when the blue is absent — and which is simultaneous with the appearing — *that alone* is the
cause of the cognition. The presence-and-absence [anvaya-vyatireka] are both present in the blue [it
tracks the real blue], and the blue existed even before the cognition (it's not produced by the
cognition). This is NOT so in the two-moon [its presence/absence doesn't track a real object]. This,
he says, is what "thus" (tathā) means.

**Objection (nanu) — the Buddhist counters:** But that "which follows the presence-and-absence, is
marked by appearing, and exists before the appearing, as the blue" — *that* cognition [the one that
tracks this] — its manifest moment (kṣaṇa) is not [a real cause]. And then the same old flaw returns:
it would still lack efficacy. [i.e. you can't get a real cause out of a momentary manifest cognition.]

**Abhinava's reply (naca):** In *your* view, this flaw doesn't even arise — because you hold that
"whatever is the cause of a cognition is what is manifested [in it]." That's perfectly non-
contradictory for you [you're an idealist — for you there's no external object to worry about]. But
consider what follows in your view: for you, the *known object*'s character of being *limited in time*
(pārimityalakṣaṇa) IS time itself; and the known [object] IS just the knowing [consciousness]; and
there's no "touch of time" in it [no real temporal relation]; and "moment" (kṣaṇa) is just a
convention, measured by the winking of an eye or some other action. So what relation does it have, and
what does "momentariness" even mean? [i.e. your own position dissolves the very notions of cause,
moment and time you need to press this objection.]

**Objection (nanu) — sarcastic:** "Now, like a poor man celebrating a festival, you've gone out of your
way to complete the *other* [Buddhist's] view, while your *own* view you've left un-filled!"

**Abhinava's reply:** And it *is* filled (pūritā) — by what follows (kṣaṇabhaṅge'pi). [i.e. my view too
is complete.]

**Step 2 — the crux (Abhinava):** Even granting momentariness (kṣaṇabhaṅga), the argument works.
If we speak in terms of the *stream* (santāna) — treating the prior and later moments as one "same"
thing by the conceit (abhimāna) of continuity — then even so: the later moment, following immediately
(after the earlier), *by that very connection* (prabandha), establishes the causal efficacy and hence
the being-ness [of the action]. This the opponent has himself established — so Abhinava turns it
against him ("here, [you've] refuted your own side"):

> "When it is said that 'the cognition-cause, helped by presence-and-absence, is the manifest blue,'
> then why is the two-moon *not* also of this kind [manifest + tracking a real object]?" — this is not
> settled [by the opponent]. For in the non-manifestation [of the prior blue / two-moon moment], there
> is no difference at all between the prior blue and the two-moon-moment [both are equally
> un-manifested prior to the cognition].

[Here Abhinava presses: if the criterion is "manifest + tracks presence/absence," then either (a) the
prior blue-moment isn't manifest and so can't be the cause — leaving the two-moon on equal footing; or
(b) if it is manifest, then the two-moon is too. Either way the Buddhist's criterion fails.]

## 3. Unresolved / uncertain (to be checked, not invented)

   own view. Interpreted here as self-completion, but the exact referent of "it" (nijā dṛṣṭiḥ = his own
   view, or the other view) is worth a second look.
3. "tat vijñānāt pūrvam abhūt eva" — the ontology of the prior blue (realist vs idealist reading).

## 4. Decision for L2
Proceed to L2. The doctrinal spine is now *understood*: Abhinava defends "efficacy = truth" by showing
that (i) manifest-ness alone is too weak (two-moon), (ii) the real criterion is manifest-ness *tracking
presence-and-absence* of a prior, and (iii) the Buddhist's own momentariness/stream concessions
actually *support* his conclusion rather than refute it. The three unresolved points above are logged,
not resolved.

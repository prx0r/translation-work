**On the truth of the action-cognitions: efficacy as the mark**

Now, Abhinavagupta turns to the criterion by which an action-cognition is to be called true. His
candidate is *efficacy*: a cognition is true when it is the cause of the effect we expect of it — when
it actually gets us to the village. But this is precisely where a Buddhist will press him, and the
exchange that follows is dense; it is worth untangling slowly.

First, the point where efficacy holds. For anything, what establishes its having causal efficacy — and
hence its reality — is that it *is the cause of a given cognition*. But notice: this cannot be said of
the object, the blue, that is manifest at the very same moment as the cognition [that perceives it]. A
cause must come before its effect; an object that is simultaneous with its cognition cannot be its
cause.

[Objection:] "Very well — then let even what is simultaneous with the manifestation count as the
cause."

[Abhinava:] That would commit us to saying that two things of equal time stand in a cause-effect
relation — which is absurd. But that is not the heart of the matter; it is only a secondary
refutation. The real point is this: it is the *being-manifest* of a thing, and nothing else, that
at that moment grounds our calling it a cause. And being-manifest is no different in the case of the
double-moon, an outright illusion. So if manifest-ness alone were the mark of truth, the double-moon
would have to be true too. Manifest-ness is therefore too weak a criterion.

What, then, is the right criterion? Take the case of the blue. The genuine cause of the cognition of
blue is that which *tracks* the blue's presence and absence — which is there whenever the blue is
there and gone whenever it is gone — and which is simultaneous with the appearing. Both the presence
and the absence are found in the blue: the blue is really perceived, and it existed before the
cognition of it arose. This is not so in the case of the double-moon. There the presence and absence
do not track any real object. This, he says, is what the word "thus" is pointing to: the cognition of
blue is true, and the cognition of the double-moon is not, because only the former is anchored in a
thing that was already there.

But the Buddhist is not finished. He rejoins: that very cognition which you say tracks the
presence-and-absence, which is marked by appearing, and which is present before the appearing, in the
form of the blue — its manifest moment is not [after all] a real cause. And then the same old flaw
returns: it too would lack efficacy.

To this Abhinavagupta makes a sharp reply — and here he turns the Buddhist's own position against him.
"In *your* view," he says, "this flaw does not even arise. For you hold that whatever is the cause of
a cognition is simply what is manifested in that cognition. For an idealist, that is perfectly
non-contradictory." But then he presses the consequence. On your view, the character of the known
object's being limited in time *is* time itself; the known object *is* nothing but the knowing
[consciousness]; there is no real "touch of time" anywhere in it; and "moment" is only a convention,
measured by the blink of an eye or some other action. So on your own premises, what relation is there
between cause and effect, and what could "momentariness" even mean? The very notions your objection
needs — moment, cause, temporal relation — are ones your own system dissolves.

The opponent, with some sarcasm, says: "Now, like a poor man putting on a festival, you have gone to
great lengths to complete the *other* side's position — while leaving your *own* position unfinished!"

Abhinavagupta's answer is that his own position is in fact completed — by what follows. And what
follows is the heart of his defence. Even granting full-blown momentariness — the doctrine that every
instant perishes — the argument still works. We may speak of a *stream* of moments and, by the conceit
of continuity, treat the earlier and later moments as one and the same thing. But even so, the later
moment, following immediately upon the earlier, by that very connectedness, establishes the causal
efficacy and hence the reality [of the action]. The opponent has himself conceded all this. So
Abhinavagupta turns the concession into a refutation:

If the criterion of truth is "being manifest and tracking the presence-and-absence of a prior real
thing," then either the prior moment of the blue is *not* manifest — in which case it cannot be the
cause, and the double-moon's moment stands on exactly the same footing — or it *is* manifest — in which
case the double-moon's moment would be manifest too, and it could not be ruled out. On the opponent's
own terms, the double-moon cannot be excluded. The Buddhist's criterion, which was meant to protect
him from the two-moon, actually cannot do so; and once it fails, the efficacy of the action-cognition
stands — and with it, its truth.

So the argument, in the end, is this: the action-cognition is true because it is efficacious; and it
is efficacious because it is anchored in a real prior state of affairs, which the illusory double-moon
is not; and the Buddhist's attempt to escape by appeal to momentariness only entangles him further,
since his own doctrine of the stream concedes exactly the connectedness that efficacy requires.

**The valid means: what establishes, and what it produces**

Abhinavagupta now turns to the heart of the relation between the known and the knower: the valid
means of cognition (pramāṇa). Everything in the world is established through a pramāṇa — nothing is
known without one — so before anything else we must determine what a pramāṇa is and what it yields.

In the world's ordinary usage, the answer seems simple. The object — the external blue, or the inner
pleasure — *by whose force* the cognition stands firm within its own fixed limit, as "this" (by its
own form) and "of-this-form" (by some further qualifying feature) — that object is called the valid
means. The blue itself, one might say, is what makes the cognition of blue valid.

But a moment's reflection undoes this. The object does not stand by its own power alone. If it did,
the blue would appear *to me* and not *to Caitra* — it would shine the same for everyone. It appears
to me, and so it stands by the power of *another*. And that other cannot be inert: an inert thing
could no more establish an appearance than a blind man could guide a blind man. So the other must be
of the nature of consciousness.

Yet mere consciousness is not enough either. An undifferentiated consciousness could never set blue
apart from yellow. What establishes the blue is a consciousness *tinted by* the blue, turned toward
the blue, shining *as* the blue — that appearance (ābhāsa) is what sets the blue before us. And the
blue it establishes is a *distinct* blue: if the blue were not distinct from consciousness, then the
very same consciousness, being one with everything, would have to be yellow too. So the appearance is
the shining of a distinct blue — even though, in the deepest sense, the blue is not other than the
great light of consciousness, the distinction appears nonetheless.

Now comes the grand unification. This whole arising — the contraction of the light by the limiting
conditions (the void, the body, the rest) — is *one single act* of the creation-power. And that one
act is precisely the illumination of the knower, the means, and the known together (pramātṛ, pramāṇa,
prameya). As the siddhapāda puts it: "the one light rises, born of means, object and knower." There is
not one power for knowing, another for the means, another for the object; it is all one light, one
manifestation.

So what, concretely, is the pramāṇa for us, the bound knowers? In the māyā-bound knower — contracted
by the body and the rest, turned inward — the appearance, as it were made distinct, faces the external
object. As long as it operates, it is tinted by the place and time of the object, changing from moment
to moment, a fresh rise each instant. And yet it is one and the same appearance, related to the one
knower, spanning endless time. That appearance — my appearance of blue — is the valid means, because it
is what does the work of producing the cognition of blue.

And what is that cognition — the fruit (pramiti)? It is, Abhinavagupta says, *the very same appearance*
turned inward: the awareness (bodhātmā) is the cognition, the fruit of the means. The means and the
fruit are not two separate things, nor are they mere synonyms. The means is the appearance facing
outward, of the nature of light; the fruit is the same appearance facing inward, of the nature of
reflexive-awareness, contracted by the tint of the object, taking the form of the word. In a single
thing the cause and its fruit are both discerned — as in the blue-light and its reflexive-awareness.
And the proof that the fruit is the reflexive-awareness is that the operation is the fruit (for the
instrument's first achievement is its operation), and the operation is non-different from the agent
being operated. So, in the primary sense, the means and the fruit are one; whatever *further* fruit
there is — the purpose served, the activity, the attainment — may be something else again. Hence the
reflexive-awareness is the fruit, and, by its force, the awareness is the means.

The reflexive-awareness, for its part, needs the word and rests in the general-form-appearance, free
of the mark of any particular place or time — that is its life. Even the bare "this" says only "what
is present, and before." So far as "Caitra stands here now" falls within the reach of words, that is
the path. It is by the force of the thing's own-marker-ness — like a direct perception of the speaker
— that one comes to rest in the specific. And this is the decisive point: **the pramāṇa's operation
is upon the appearance, and the appearance alone.** It does not seize the bare thing apart from all
manifestation; it works in and through the appearance.

## Fidelity note

- **Preserved:** the two kinds of relation; the ordinary sense of pramāṇa (object by whose force the
  cognition stands as "this/of-this-form"); the reflection that the object doesn't stand by its own
  power (or it would appear to everyone); the other must be conscious (blind-leading-blind); mere
  consciousness not enough (must be tinted by blue); the distinct blue (else all would be one
  consciousness = yellow too); the one creation-power illuminating knower-means-known; the siddhapāda
  quote; the māyā-knower's appearance as the pramāṇa; the cognition = the same appearance turned
  inward, reflexive-awareness, word-form; means and fruit non-different in the primary sense; the
  further fruit (purpose/attainment) may be other; vimarśa = fruit, bodha = means; the word and the
  general-form as the reflexive-awareness's life; the operation on appearance alone.
- **Added (SUPPLIED, recoverable):** "nothing is known without one" (glosses pramāṇādhīnā vastusiddhi);
  "the blue would appear to me and not to Caitra" (makes the subject-relativity explicit); "an inert
  thing could no more establish an appearance than a blind man could guide a blind man" (unpacks the
  andhenāndha image); "It does not seize the bare thing apart from all manifestation" (states the
  ābhāsamātre conclusion in one line). Interpretive unpacking, not new doctrine.
- **NOT resolved:** the full list of limiting upādhis (śūnya/deha/prāṇa) beyond what's stated; the
  exact force of the pā. vā. 3.308 citation. Logged, not invented.

**Verdict:** understood and readable. The pramāṇavimarśa opens with a clean, coherent argument: the
pramāṇa is the appearance of a distinct object in consciousness (standing firm by its own conscious
power); its fruit is the reflexive-awareness of that same appearance turned inward; means and fruit are
one in the primary sense; and the means operates on appearance alone. This sets up the ten kārikās on
per-appearance operation that follow.

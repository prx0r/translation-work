**The spontaneity of the Lord: the self-aware light**

This is where the whole Īśvarapratyabhijñāvivṛtivimarśinī begins — with the spontaneity, the
svācchandya, of the Lord. And the doctrine is announced in its fullness from the first lines. The blue
and the yellow and the jar are not things that a light shines upon from outside; they shine forth
*as* the light, without abandoning their own being, and are one with the manifestation itself. This —
that the world shines as the consciousness, and that the consciousness shines as the world — is the
Lord's spontaneity.

The Lord is of the nature of consciousness. He shines forth as the whole variety of knowers and knowns,
with a body that appears as if it were more than consciousness even though it is not more; and — this is
decisive — he is *aware* of shining. The image Abhinavagupta uses is the mirror. A mirror reflects the
wall, the house, the elephant, the horse, the person, the pot, the potter — all the distinctions of
part and whole, support and supported, cause and effect — without being more than its own smooth
surface. But the mirror does not know that it reflects. That is its only limit; it is inert. The Lord
is the mirror that *knows*: he shines forth as all things, and he is aware that he does so, because
consciousness culminates in reflexive-awareness. The light that shines is the light that knows itself
shining.

And this answers the obvious objection: "then it would always shine." For the "always" has no
independent time against which it could fail. What we call time is just the sun's movement — the
shining of things in their succession. To ask "why does the world shine as the sun moves?" is like
asking "why does the blue not shine as yellow?" It is the nature of the light to shine; there is no
time outside it that could make it shine or not. So the Lord is: *śaśvat*, un-cut by time; *acchanna*,
never becoming other than his own form; *svaccha*, clear — able to appear as more than himself while
being only himself; and *svacchanda*, spontaneous — free of all dependence on another, resting in his
own pulsation.

And so the opening of the work is a prostration. The author bows to Śiva — the param-eśvara who removes
the delusion of the world, who is the essence of all good, the bliss and the liberation. And the
prostration, Abhinavagupta explains, is not an empty word. It is the samāveśa, the entry: the
abandoning of the bound knower's conceit, the subordination of the "I" of the world, and the rising of
the self-aware consciousness. Even the word "namaḥ," the bowing, does its work not by being offered as
a bare sound but by pleasing the Lord — as the oblation's svāhā kindles the flame and gives to the
deity.

So the whole teaching is here, in the first opening: the Lord is the self-aware light, the mirror that
knows, the spontaneity that shines as the world and is aware of shining; and the recognition of this —
the entry into this self-aware Śiva — is the whole of the liberation the work will unfold.

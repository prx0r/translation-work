**The homage that is two-resting, and who is worthy to make it**

The work opens with a prostration — and Abhinavagupta takes care to say what that prostration is. It
is not a bending of the body, nor an empty sound. It is a *two-resting* homage: it rests at once in the
Lord, whom it exalts, and in the devotee, whom it subordinates. The words of homage — "jaya," "victory,"
"namaḥ," "I bow," "I worship" — all move by one of these two directions, but each must culminate in the
other. The "victory" of the Lord, to be real, requires the devotee's self-subordination; the "I bow" of
the devotee, to be real, must rest in the Lord's excess. The two — the exalting and the subordinating —
are one act, two aspects, made manifest by the single word of homage before the whole assembly.

And the attaining of the Lord, the commentary tells us, is the attainment of *dāsya* — of
servant-ness. And the servant-ness is extremely hard to get: that is what "kathaṃcit," "in some way,"
means. It is not a title; it is the very heart of the eligibility. And it is fruitful of the greatest
good — for in becoming the servant of the Lord one attains the Lord's own excess, and all the fortunes
(dāsyalakṣmī) follow from that servant-ness, granted by the Lord's grace. It is this that makes the
devotee one who has attained all.

The homage, moreover, is made in the mind and the speech — the mind-awareness and the word-essence —
never in the inert body. "Even by speech," the text says, and by the "even" we understand "and by the
body." But the prostration proper is the offering of the undivided mind-and-speech, which is the
consciousness-power itself; and in offering that, the devotee offers the whole world to the Lord. As the
teaching says: "he should offer his own self." The physical prostration — the bowing, the eight-limbed
salutation — is only the upacāra, the visible mark by which the inner offering is inferred, not the
primary act.

And so the question of who is worthy. The objection arises: "the eligibility suits the explainer, who
has attained; but how does it suit the mere listeners?" Abhinavagupta's answer is that the listener and
the explainer are not two separate undertakings: the recognition is one, and the servant-ness is one. It
is by this that the Lord's favor is won — the recognition is preceded by, and inferred from, the Lord's
favor. And this is where the Gītā's four kinds of devotees come in — the distressed, the seeker of
knowledge, the seeker of wealth, and the knower. All of them, in their several gradations, are worthy of
the servant-ness and of the recognition. The door is not narrow in the way of the world's ranks; it is
narrow only in the way of the heart — for what is required is the self-subordination, the dāsya, and
that, once attained, draws the Lord near.

**The own-self: the one consciousness, whose body is the world**

The kārikā says that the own-self alone is the Lord. But what is this "own-self"? Does it reach the
whole world — the inert things, the void, everything? The objector presses hard on this. If the
maheśvara is the self of the blue, then he must be the self of the yellow too; and then how can the
world be divided — "this is the blue, this is the yellow"? The answer turns on what "own-self" really
means.

The "own" in "own-self" (svātmā) is not restricted to anything in particular; so it is the self's own —
the inner, non-other-dependent, un-conditioned relation. And what is that? It is consciousness. For
the conscious things, he is plainly the one self. But for the inert — the lump of clay, the blue — how
can they have one self, since separate things seem to have separate natures? Here Abhinavagupta shows
that the inert, in its supposed inertia, has no real being at all. If the clay is truly in itself, is
it or is it not? If it were not, nothing would suffer; if it did its effect, so what; if it shone,
what is its own glory? The "it shines for me, for Caitra" fails too — for the perception belongs to
the perceiver, not to the thing; and "by the perceiver it shines" is a new-fangled reason that would
make the white make the yellow white. In truth, the thing has no being apart from its being-manifested;
and being-manifested is the consciousness's own act. So the blue and the yellow are not things with
their own separate bodies; they are *awareness-forms* (bodhātmānaḥ) — and their own-self, no less than
ours, is the maheśvara.

What of the void, the mere nothing? If it is cognized at all, then the maheśvara is its self too; if
it is not cognized, then it is nothing, and no one's self is at issue. The test is always the same: is
it cognized? That is the whole answer — the "being-cognized" is what makes anything the self of the
one consciousness.

And so the division of the world. If the maheśvara is the self of the blue and of the yellow alike,
how is "this is the blue, this is the yellow" possible? Abhinavagupta's answer is that the division is
not a division of the self. The whole known world is the *body* of the consciousness-self. Just as my
body, though an object of my knowledge, is nonetheless "I" — not metaphorically, but literally, by the
un-abandoned support of the self — so the whole world is "I" to the consciousness. The body's being
"I" is no mere figure of speech (as when we call a tool by its owner's name); even those who think
themselves quite separate from one another feel the body as "I." So the consciousness is
world-form (viśvarūpā); the blue and the yellow are not cut off from it as from a thing outside, but
are its very forms, as the space of the jar and the space of the cloth are the one space.

The upshot: the own-self is the one consciousness, whose body is the whole world. The blue and the
yellow, the inert and the void, are all awareness-forms; their own-self is the maheśvara. The division
of the world is the division of what appears within the one light, not a split in the light itself.
And this one — the knower, the doer, the self of all — is the Lord: the jñātṛ-kartṛ, who knows and
acts, and whose very being is to be the self of everything.

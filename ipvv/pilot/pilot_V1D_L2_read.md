**The eternal knower: "I was never not"**

Memory seems to bind the self to the past — to what the self has itself experienced. But then the
objection arises, and it is a sharp one. "I was not at the time of the Rāmāyaṇa," the reader says.
"Vālmīki composed it long ago, and I was not there to experience it; so how can the self, which
remembers only what it has experienced, be the eternal knower?"

Abhinavagupta answers that the objection mistakes the body for the self. It is true that *this body*
was not at the time of the Rāmāyaṇa — but that is not the self. The "I"-recollection, the aham-vimarśa,
is of the consciousness-nature, and it is eternal. And here is the crucial move: if memory reaches only
what has been experienced, then even the Vālmīki of the Rāmāyaṇa was an experiencer; and the āgama
itself, by which we learn of that time, presupposes an experiencer. What flashes in the "we were not" —
the past-time-appearance, in the perfect tense — is itself a cognition; and since it is not the
experience-form (else the past would flash like the yogin's present), it must be memory. And memory, by
its very nature, presupposes a prior experience. So every memory, even the memory of the Rāmāyaṇa, points
back to an experiencer who was there.

This is where the eternal knower enters. The pure consciousness-mere, being free of the tint of time,
cannot by itself bear the memory; the memory is of the body-and-the-rest-tinted. But the tint of the
prior body is distinct from the tint of this body — so the memory-ness, if pinned to the body, would not
be clear (as the young man who was never a child would have no clear memory of childhood). The clear
memory requires the firm conceit of the experiencer — and that conceit, in its root, is the self that
was there. And that self, being consciousness, is not cut by time.

So the manifestation of the self is not un-precedented (apūrva). It does not begin to shine at some
moment; it was always shining. The being-ness of the self rests in its manifestation, and its being was
never otherwise. Hence the "un-precedented manifestation" (apūrva-prakāśa) that we seem to see — the
new appearance of things in time — presupposes, and rests upon, the eternal knower. The body's
appearance is un-precedented, but only against the background of the eternal, always-manifested
param-essence knower. And that knower is the param-eśvara, who is never un-precedented in his
manifestation; and all the knowers are un-divided from him. So, in truth, there is no un-precedented
manifestation at all.

This is the point of the Gītā verse that Abhinavagupta cites: "For truly I was never not… nor shall
we not be." The self — the param-essence knower — is un-cut by all three times: past, present, and
future. It did not come into being, and it will not pass away. And because all the knowers are one
with it, the same is true, in the deepest sense, of them all. The un-precedented manifestation may
appear in the māyā-bound knower, but it rests in the eternal knower. The pramāṇa — the valid means by
which we know — is thus grounded, in the end, in this one eternal knower, who was never not, and who is
the self of all.

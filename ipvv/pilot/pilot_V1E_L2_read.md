**The self is the Lord: what the recognition does**

The kārikā declares that the self alone is the Lord. And at once a sharp objection arises. A valid
means of knowledge, it is said, establishes what was *not* established before — it serves the practical
dealings of the world. But the Lord-self, on our own showing, is already established. So what could a
pramāṇa possibly do here? What is there left for it to prove?

Abhinavagupta's answer turns this objection around. For an inert object, even an established one, the
pramāṇa does indeed make a new attainment — it brings out some part that was not yet grasped, at least
the manifestation and the designation (prakhyā and upākhyā) of the thing in our dealing with it. But in
the self — the Lord — there is nothing un-attained to be established. The manifestation and the
designation are already there; without them, not even a trace of a thing could be known at all. So the
pramāṇa here is not establishing something new.

What, then, is it doing? It is *foregrounding* what is already present but hidden. The recognition
(pratyabhijñā) does not bring the Lord into being; it brings the Lord, who was always near and always
present, into the *foreground of our own awareness*. As Abhinavagupta says, what is hidden and standing
close by is shown as primary, by the coming-together of the heart. The recognition makes the
manifestation and the designation of the self-as-Lord no longer an instrument lost among the things of
the body and the world, but the primary thing that we see. It is the turning of the attention — the
"see, see" — upon what was always before us.

And this recognition is the very content of the whole teaching. After the upoddhāta, the opening, the
whole śāstra that is to come has, as its to-be-said content, the recognition — which is the
jñāna-kriyā, the knowledge-and-action power, made firm by the conviction (dṛḍhaniścaya) that arises from
it. The two powers — knowledge and action — are determined at length throughout the work, and the firm
conviction in the self-as-Lord is their fruit.

So the point is this: the self is the Lord, and he is already established — not to be newly proved, but
to be *recognized*. The pramāṇa does not reach out to a distant, un-known thing; it turns the light of
attention upon the one who was always the knower and the known, the one standing near in the very act
of knowing. The recognition is not the acquisition of something new; it is the foregrounding of the
self, the Lord, who was never absent. And this, the firm conviction in the self-as-Lord, is what the
whole teaching sets before us.

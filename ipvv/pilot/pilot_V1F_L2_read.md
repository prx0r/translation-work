**The self is the Lord: against the Buddhist no-self**

The upoddhāta, the preliminary of the whole work, must first clear the ground — by showing who denies
the self's lordship and why their denial fails. Not everyone is a real opponent. The materialist, the
Cārvāka, cannot even argue: having no means of proof of their own, they cannot refute the other side
without first adopting the very conventions of proof they reject. The Sāṃkhya, with its many selves or
its one self with hidden lordship, is not wholly far from us. The real opponent — the one who must be
met head-on — is the **Buddhist**, the Sautrāntika, who denies the self outright. It is his no-self that
the eleven verses of this vimarśa set out and answer.

The Buddhist's attack is twofold. First, he denies the self that we claim to perceive: "the self is
not seen," he says, "only the momentary cognitions flash by, with their own-mark appearances." Second,
he denies the self that we might infer: memory and the synthesis, he holds, are otherwise established —
they are only the work of the impressions in the stream, with no abiding owner behind them. So the
self, whether taken as perceived or as inferred, is rejected; and with it, he concludes, the whole
apparatus of the knowledge-action relation — the very lordship we ascribe to the self — collapses, for
there is no one to be the knower and the doer.

Abhinavagupta meets him on both fronts. To the denial of the perceived self, he answers with the
"I"-cognition itself (ahaṃpratyaya): the self is not some object hidden behind the perceptions — it is
present, unmistakably, in the very act of saying "I." The "I" is not a thing seen among others; it is
the seeing itself, that which is present in every cognition, never itself absent. The ādi — the stream
of the senses, the breath, the knowledge — all presuppose this "I" that runs through them. The Buddhist's
"the self is not seen" fails: the self is the seer, and the seer is never not seen, for he is the
seeing.

And the memory and the synthesis, far from being otherwise established, themselves require the abiding
self. A memory reaches across the break of experience and says "I saw this before" — and only an
abiding self could hold the before and the after together in the single "I." The stream of moments
cannot do this; moments have no thread by which to re-collect. So memory and the synthesis, which the
Buddhist thought freed him from the self, in truth demand the self. And the knowledge-action relation —
the knowing and the doing — is precisely this self's lordship: the one who knows and the one who acts
is the self, and that is what it is to be the Lord.

So the upoddhāta's defense is complete in outline. The Cārvāka cannot argue, the Sāṃkhya is near; the
real opponent, the Buddhist no-self, is met and answered. The self is established even by the
"I"-cognition; memory and the synthesis require the abiding self; and the knowledge-action relation —
the knowing and the doing of the one who says "I" — is the self's lordship. The self is real, and the
self is the Lord.

**The cognition cannot be a thing: the dilemma of the consciousness-form**

The Buddhist had denied the self. But he had also, in a different mood, offered a reason for the
self's lordship — "by the union of knowledge and action." To stop even this, Abhinavagupta turns the
same logic against the cognition itself. Whatever the opponent says cognition is, it cannot stand.
And he forces the choice with a single dilemma, put in the seventh kārikā: if the cognition is of the
consciousness-form, then it must be eternal, like the self; but if it is inert, then it cannot reveal
any object at all. There is no third option.

Take the first horn. The consciousness-nature is, by its very marker, *self-manifestation* — it is the
light that knows itself. And self-manifestation is directly opposed to being-an-object, to being
manifested by another. A thing that is manifest by another is caught in place and time — it is here or
there, now or then. But the self-manifest light is free of all such being-caught. If the cognition were
truly of the consciousness-form, it could not be the momentary, place-and-time-bound thing the Buddhist
takes it to be; it would have to be eternal and omnipresent, exactly like the self. And then the
Buddhist's whole picture — a stream of fleeting cognitions, each a separate momentary quality — falls
apart. The cognition would not be a momentary flash; it would be the eternal light itself.

The Buddhist tries to escape by saying: "the cognition is not the self's own form; it is a quality of
the self, made by the union with the mind." But this, Abhinavagupta shows, fails too. For what we call
the cognition is the manifestation of the blue and the rest; and if that manifestation is only an
occasional quality, absent when its cause is absent, then the self, apart from it, is simply not
manifesting the blue. The cognition, taken as a separate thing, has no place to stand. Whether you call
it consciousness-form or a quality, it dissolves.

The second horn is no better. If the cognition is inert — a dead thing, like the objects — then how
could it reveal anything? An inert thing does not shine; it is shone upon. It cannot be the
manifestation of the blue, for manifestation is not a property of the inert. So the inert cognition
cannot account for the one thing it is supposed to explain — the appearing of objects.

And beneath both horns lies a deeper point. If the cognition were a separate thing, caught in place and
time, it would have to be held and supported by something; but the self-manifest light cannot be held
or covered by another — the covered thing, we might say, could not bear another's support. So the whole
idea of a cognition as an object supported by a self collapses, and with it the Buddhist's reason for
the self's lordship.

What survives the collapse is the light itself. The cognition is not a thing to be placed and timed; it
is the self-manifestation, which is the self. And this is what leads Abhinavagupta to the Sāṃkhya's
buddhi — the intellect — which the Sāṃkhya makes the locus of the cognitions. For even there, the same
dilemma will hold: if the buddhi is consciousness-form it is eternal, if inert it cannot manifest. The
cognition is not a quality to be located anywhere; it is the self-manifest light, which is the self —
and the union of knowledge and action, far from being a reason for lordship that the opponent can
offer, is simply the self's own nature, the knowing and the acting of the one light.

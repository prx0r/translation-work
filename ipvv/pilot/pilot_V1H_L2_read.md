**Enjoyment is the meeting of two reflections**

What is it to enjoy — to experience an object? The schools have their answers: the Sāṃkhya makes the
cognition a transformation of matter; the Vaiśeṣika makes it a quality of the self joined to the object
by a mysterious relation called inherence (samavāya). Abhinavagupta cuts through all of this with a
single image: enjoyment is the *union of two reflections* — the reflection of consciousness and the
reflection of the object — meeting in the intellect, and fused by the ego into the one act "I know
this."

Consider what happens when you know the blue. The intellect (buddhi) bears two reflections: the
reflection of consciousness — the light itself, as it is present in the act of knowing — and the
reflection of the blue, the object. The ego (ahaṅkāra) mixes them into the single conceit "I know
this," and that conceit — the two reflections joined, determined by the intellect, resting in the one
consciousness — is the enjoyment. The self, in itself, is not really an enjoyer; it is
consciousness-mere. It is the ego that makes it appear as one who enjoys. And this is not a mere figure
of speech in the loose sense: if it were, then by the infinite division of knowers, the same body could
be dealt with at once as fire and water, as contradictory things, in a single moment — which is absurd.
The enjoyment is real, but real as this union of reflections in the one consciousness.

Now the Vaiśeṣika says: the cognition is a quality of the self, and it is joined to the object by
inherence (samavāya). But Abhinavagupta's point is that the mere *relation* does all the work. If the
cognition-quality is other than the self, then how is it enjoyed? "By inherence," the Vaiśeṣika says.
But then the relation alone is the cause of the enjoyment-form — and that is precisely our point. Here
too there is a relation: the consciousness-reflection, the object-reflection, the ego's mixing, the
intellect's determining — the union in the one support. And the union is enough. Whether you call it
conjunction (saṃyoga) or inherence (samavāya) matters not at all; once the thing is understood, the
word is a mere label. And if the conjunction's beginning is not to be found in the prior dissolutions,
then the conjunction, being beginningless, is not distinct from the inherence. Either way, in every
view, the enjoyment-dealing is *relation-made* — saṃsargakṛta.

So the deep point is this. Enjoyment is not a transaction between a self and a thing across a gap; it
is the meeting, within the one light, of the light and the object's reflection, fused by the ego into
"I know this." The relation — whatever we name it — is the whole of it. And this is why the 
consciousness-reflection and the object-reflection, wherever they meet in the intellect, stand in the
support-supported relation, even to the point where one might call it inherence — without any
contradiction. The enjoyment of the world is, in the end, the self-luminous light meeting its own
reflections in the one act of knowing.

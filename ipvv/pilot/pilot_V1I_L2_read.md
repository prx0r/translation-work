**Action is the ordered operation, not a thing that moves**

The opponent had offered the self's lordship as the union of knowledge and action. We have seen that
knowledge cannot be a separate thing in his view. Now Abhinavagupta shows that *action* cannot be a
separate thing either. Kārikā 9 makes the point: the action of a body is just the coming-to-be of this
or that place; it is not some hidden cause (the unseen, adṛṣṭa) moving it; and it is not one — for
order does not belong to a single thing.

But wait — if action is not a movement, what is it? The word-knowers, the grammarians, refuse to say
that the body-less self moves. They locate the self's agency only in the union of knowledge, desire,
and effort. So the action that belongs to the self must be defined differently. Its marker,
Abhinavagupta says, is this: the operation of the agent that brings about the fruit — the means,
related to the doer, or inhering in it, or its very self — whose form is such that its parts are
prior-and-later, standing in order. Action is the ordered operation. And the gurus' verses make this
vivid: "as long as the established or un-established is spoken of as the to-be-established, being of
the resting-order-form, it is perceived as that action." The mass of order-born parts, unified by the
mind, is what we call action. "White is whitened" — the whiteness appears as a prior-later order, and
that is the declaration of action. So action is not a thing that moves; it is the ordered self-shaping
of a nature.

And this action is in the self. "He desires, he knows, he strives" — these are the prior-later dharmas
of the one self, perceived in their order; and this ordered operation, Abhinavagupta notes, is accepted
by the very thinkers who hold the self to be the Lord. The guru's words: "such is the will, such the
knowledge, such the action." The will, the knowledge, the action — these are not three separate things
attached to a self; they are the one self's ordered operation, its desiring and knowing and striving,
held together as the single action.

So the action, like the cognition, cannot be broken into momentary flashes or hidden causes. It is the
one, ordered operation of the self — the will that knows and acts. And this is why the union of
knowledge and action is not a reason that the opponent can offer for lordship; it is the self's own
nature. The self that desires, knows, and strives — in that single ordered act — is the Lord. The
union of knowledge and action is not a quality added to the self; it is the self, shining as the 
ordered operation of the one who wills and knows and does.

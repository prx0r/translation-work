**The reply: the cognition is self-cognition**

The Buddhist's attack has run its course — the self, the cognition, the action, the relation all
denied, and "thence the agent too is constructed." Now, in the third vimarśa, the established doctrine
replies. And Abhinavagupta's first move is a shrewd concession. "It is true," he begins — and this is
the characteristic gesture of the reflective thinker, granting the opponent what is sound in order to
sharpen what is not. It is true, he says, that the cognition cannot be a separate thing in the way the
Sāṃkhya or the Kāṇāda suppose; that much the Buddhist has rightly seen. But — and here is the turn — the
reason the cognition cannot be a separate thing is precisely because it is *self-cognition*
(svasaṃvid). It is not a quality attached to a self; it is the self's own luminous awareness of itself.
And this self-cognition is the very self the Buddhist thought he had abolished.

Consider memory. When you remember, the prior experience does not stand before you as a new object; it
shines in the single act of the "I" that remembers. The cognition, even in memory, is self-cognition —
it is aware of itself, not of some detached past thing. The Buddhist thought memory freed him from the
self; but memory itself is self-luminous. And if the construction, the determination, were all error —
as the Buddhist would have it — then every determination would fall, and the dealing of the world would
collapse. The Buddhist cannot have it both ways. The memory, the determination, the cognition all
presuppose the self-luminous awareness that is aware of itself in each act.

So the siddhānta's reply rests on the self-luminous dṛk — the seeing that is aware of itself. The "I"
is not constructed; it is that without which no cognition, no memory, no construction could shine at
all. The cognition is self-cognition: it is the light that knows itself, and this light is the self —
the self that the Buddhist denied and Abhinavagupta here restores. And because this self-cognition is
self-luminous and free, it is the Lord. The one who is aware of himself in every act of knowing is the
one who knows and acts — the self, the Lord, whose cognition is his self-cognition and whose power
reaches through the whole of memory and action.

The jñāna-vicāra — the examination of the cognition — thus begins the reply. The Buddhist is granted
his insight (the cognition is not a separate thing) and then outflanked: it is not a thing because it
is the self-luminous awareness, which is the self. From here the siddhānta will build — showing that
this self-cognition is the memory, the power of removal, and ultimately the Lord who knows and acts.

**The self-luminous seeing: how the yogin knows another's mind**

We have said the cognition is self-cognition, and the self is self-luminous. But this seems to close
the door on a striking fact — the yogin's knowledge of another's mind. If the self is not an object,
how can the yogin perceive another's cognition with perfect clarity? And if the cognition is
self-luminous only in its own stream, could it not, after all, be manifested by some other cognition?

The answer to the first is that the yogin's knowledge of another is a *perception*, not an inference.
It is the yogipratyakṣa. By the discipline of the yogin — the holding, the meditating, the samādhi —
one comes, in the union with the other, to meditate the non-difference of the self from the other. And
that meditation, following the truth of the thing (for in truth there is unity, and the conceit of
difference is māyā-made), makes the other's cognition shine forth directly, clearly, as a thing seen —
not inferred, not concluded. As the yoga-sūtra says, the knowledge of another's cognition comes by the
saṃyama. The other's passions, too, are manifested in this seeing; but in their very experience the
yogin and the other are one, so the experience shines as self-luminous, not as an object spread before
a detached observer.

And this is the point that matters. The other's cognition, thus seen, is a direct seeing
(sākṣātkāra) — not a conclusion about another stream. And the self that sees, and the self that is
seen, are, in that act, one. So the "being-manifested-by-another" — the picture of one cognition
standing as an object before another — has no place even here. The other knower too is not manifested
by another; he is self-luminous, and in the yogin's perception the two are one.

Now the second objection: "the cognition is self-luminous in its own stream, but could it not, with
respect to another knower, be manifested by another cognition? After all, the same woman gives pleasure
to her lover and pain to her rival — the same thing, different effects, no contradiction." Abhinavagupta
answers with the unity of the knower: "he experienced, I have experienced." The self-cognition rests in
the one knower. The "he" and the "I" are not two streams that might objectify each other; they are the
one self, which remembers its own experience. The cognition is not a thing to be passed from one stream
to another as an object; it is the one self-luminous awareness, aware of itself, and of nothing
else as an object.

So the dṛk — the seeing — is self-manifest alone. It is never the object of another cognition, because
it is the very light in which all cognitions shine, and it is one. The yogin's perception of another is
not the exception that breaks this; it is its confirmation — for in that perception, the two are one,
and the other's cognition is the yogin's own self-luminous awareness. And the Buddhist's picture of
separate streams, each objectifying the other, is refuted. The seeing is self-luminous; the self is
one; and this self-luminous, one seeing is the self — and the Lord.

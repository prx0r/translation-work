**The determination cannot reach outside**

The Buddhist, having lost the self, the cognition, the action, and the relation, falls back on one
last notion: the *determination* (adhyavasāya) — the act by which, he thinks, the cognition is taken
to establish an external thing. Abhinavagupta now cuts this last thread. The determination, he shows,
cannot establish anything outside.

Consider how the "external" seems to appear. It is, Abhinavagupta says, only the strength of the firm
practice-impression that makes the appearance seem drawn to an external object. From the very first,
the appearance shining in the consciousness and in the determination is taken as external — but only
for the slow-minded, in the illusion and the diseased-eye case. He who knows the cause of the
appearance does not determine its externality at all. The child who cries in astonishment, "the trees
on the far bank rush against the current!" is told: "it is not so — the trees do not rush; it is only
seen so." The manifestation-dharma alone is this. The seeming outwardness is a manner of the seeing,
not a thing established.

And the determination itself, Abhinavagupta presses, is error-form. In every determination — even in
the most ordinary case — there is this error-form, this drawing of the appearance outward. So the
external, which the error-form determination was supposed to establish, has no standing in that very
part: for the part is inert, and an inert thing cannot establish. And in the part that is not inert —
the non-construction, the pure self-experience — there the establishment is not made either, for that
part shines only its own form and its own self-appearance. So the determination's object-establishing
power fails on both sides. The non-erroneous part, the self-experience, is un-divided from the
memory-cognition — it is not a separate thing reaching out to an external — and so it is not
external-natured.

The Buddhist tries once more: "let the determination be both erroneous and establishing — what is the
harm?" But this too fails, for the reason is the inertness: an inert thing cannot establish. And the
last attempt — "as the fire burns the wood though inert, so the determination establishes the object;
why this question of inert or conscious?" — is met: the establishing is in the self; the experience,
with the manifestation at its head, is the establishing, and it is self-luminous, not a thing that
reaches a thing.

So the whole attempt to find a real external established by the determination collapses. The
determination is only the manifestation-form in the self; the external is only drawn-to by the
impression, never established; and the self-experience, un-divided from the memory-cognition, is the
self-luminous awareness alone. The cognition never establishes a thing outside itself, because there
is nothing outside the self-luminous awareness for it to reach. The self is one, self-luminous, and
all — the memory, the determination, the experience — are its own shining.

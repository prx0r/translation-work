**The one who is the whole world: the close of the first volume**

The cognitions, we have seen, are self-luminous and one with the "I." But how, the question arises, do
they not take the form of the "I" when they appear as the objects — the blue, the yellow, the jar? The
answer is that their seeming excess over the "I" — their non-pervasion, their non-eternity, their being
caught in the place and time of the object — is made only by the object-facing condition (upādhi). The
difference of the object, Abhinavagupta says, is a mere conceit (abhimāna), made by the māyā-power; and
even in the state of māyā, the deep un-dividedness of the object from the "I" is un-broken. For māyā is
the Lord's own power — and a power does not cover the power-holder. The māyā cannot hide the Lord's own
form, because it is his own shining. So the whole world, even the seemingly external, is un-divided
from the "I": the one whose body is consciousness, with the inner-made, infinite world-form, possessed
of the knowledge and the rest of the powers.

This is the great vision toward which the volume has been building. The Lord is the one whose body is
the whole world — the inner-made, the outer-form, all of it, un-lost in the least, made by his freedom
and resting in the one knower. The world, counted in the four, the six, the sixteen, the twenty-four,
the thirty-six categories and more, is his body. There is nothing outside the consciousness; the māyā
is his power, not a veil over him.

And the synthesis (anusandhāna) — the act by which the many cognitions are gathered into one — is
itself the consciousness. The objection: "you said the memory's arising is the world's standing; but
here you say 'if the synthesis were not,' and call it consciousness-form — how?" Abhinavagupta answers:
the synthesis of all the knowledges, mutually, *is* the consciousness-tattva. By the co-reference, by
the following-ness, the one-becoming of even the seemingly separate cognitions is the consciousness
alone, turned inward — nothing else. The mixer of the fragments is not other than the consciousness.
So "from the synthesis is the world's standing" — true; but that synthesis, being of the one-object-
ness, is not possible by any other way; it depends, by the remainder, on the consciousness-form alone.

And so the first volume comes to its close. The cognitions are one with the "I"; the māyā is the
Lord's power, which does not cover him; the synthesis is the consciousness alone; and the Lord — the
one whose body is the whole world, possessed of the knowledge, the memory, and the power of removal —
is established as the self-contained consciousness-non-duality. The one who knows, who remembers, who
removes — these are his powers, and they are all his. This is the maheśvara; and with his
establishment, the foundation of the whole Īśvarapratyabhijñā is laid. The recognition of this one —
the Lord, who is the self and the whole world — is what the work will unfold.

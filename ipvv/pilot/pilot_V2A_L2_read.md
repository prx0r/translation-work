**Memory as the Lord's power: how the "I" holds time together**

The jñānādhikāra has established knowledge; now it takes up memory. And here Abhinavagupta makes a
startling claim. The ordinary view — the view of the schools — is that memory is only the revival of an
impression after the experience is gone. When the experience is destroyed, a mere trace remains, and
memory is the stirring of that trace. But Abhinavagupta rejects this. Memory, he insists, is itself a
*knowledge*; and far from being a pale echo of experience, it is the **Lord's own power** — the
un-broken manifestation by which the past and the present are held together in the single "I."

Consider what memory actually does. It brings the previously-experienced object back before the mind;
the object appears, again, in the knower. If memory were only an impression's revival, this would be
inexplicable — for how could a mere trace present an object? The truth is that memory is the un-broken
manifestation of the experienced object: the experience and its object are carried forward into the
memory as one. In memory, the experience does not stand apart as a separate object; it is the very act
of knowing that returns. Even the yogin's knowledge of another, Abhinavagupta notes, does not appear as
a detached object — it is the one awareness continuing. The separation that memory seems to make between
the past cognition and the present one is only a construction (kālpanika), not a real division. And
ultimately, the remembered, the memory, and the rememberer — and by the same token the seen, the seeing,
and the seer — all rest in the one consciousness-essence.

Now the "I" itself. The "aham" shines in two directions at once. As "the seer of the blue" it is turned
inward; but the same "I, thus" is also turned outward toward the blue. The experience is the outward-
facing blue-knowledge. And here is the freedom: by the freedom of the awareness, one first construes,
within, "I would see the blue," and then the blue appears without. The inner construing precedes the
outer manifestation, as the seed precedes the sprout. So the object, tinted by its place and its time,
enters the knower and the experience together; and in the memory "I saw the blue then," the three — the
I, the blue, the then — appear entered into that one act.

This is the key to time itself. When we say "the jar stands, will stand, stood," what makes this
possible is the un-brokenness of the manifestation — the awareness that holds the jar's appearances
together across the moments. The experience is the gate through which time is entered. As long as the
awareness is not touched by time, there is no time-dealing at all; but when it manifests the jar in its
past, present, and future, time appears. And the one who says "I" across all of this is the same — the
one self, who saw, who remembers, who will see. Memory is the thread by which the "I" holds time
together, and it is, in the end, the Lord's own power shining as the un-broken awareness.

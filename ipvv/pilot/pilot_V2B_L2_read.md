**The self-luminous cognition: how memory reaches the past, and the yogin reaches the other**

Two puzzles press on the account of memory. The first is the pastness of the past. We remember what
happened yesterday — but how, if the manifestation of consciousness has no touch of time at all? The
answer is that the pastness is not a real quality of the object; it is the *being-previously-manifested*.
The one light, contracting under the limiting conditions of place and time — "yesterday, thirsty, with
that friend, I saw this jar un-ripe" — appears as the past. The manifestation is cut by these upādhis;
and what we call the māyā-word, the realm of ordinary experience, is precisely this contraction of the
light by the conditions. The memory is the re-apprehension of what was previously manifested; and the
cognition of the object, so cut by the past experience, is itself a fresh act of experience. There is
nothing ghostly in memory: it is the one light, narrowed to a past moment, shining again.

The second puzzle is the yogin's perception of another's mind. The yogin is the one who has attained
unity with the supreme knower; for him, all objects are un-cut by time, present in his own self as "I"
or as "I-this." How, then, does one particular appearance arise for him, and with its past-time-
specificity? Here Abhinavagupta gives the key: time is the order of appearing and not-appearing. The
body, flashing and fading moment by moment, is what gives the endless difference of time. Those who
take the body as the knower are bound in the chain of time, and can never see past or future. But the
yogin, by the teaching and the sustained meditation, thins the body — and then the other's cognition
shines in its own place.

And this leads to the deepest point: the nature of cognition itself. The cognition is
self-cognition (svasaṃvitti). Its own nature — that which never deviates, the marker neither over- nor
under-pervading — is to be self-luminous, the own-awareness that distinguishes it utterly from the
inert. The jar is not self-aware; it is only what it is. But cognition, in its very being, is aware of
itself. And precisely because it is self-cognition, it is also free to manifest the *other*: the same
luminous awareness that knows itself can, in its freedom, shine upon another's cognition. The inert
cannot do this; only the self-luminous awareness can. So the yogin's knowledge of another is nothing
other than the other's own-cognition, self-luminous, which the yogin — by the purity and the un-opposed
spreading of his own cognition — comes to know.

The upshot is a single, elegant thesis. Cognition is self-cognition: it is the own-awareness that is
the very nature of consciousness, distinguishing it from the inert. And because it is self-cognition, it
is also other-manifesting — it can reach the past in memory, and reach the other in the yogin's
perception. Memory and clairvoyance are not exceptions to the nature of cognition; they are the
overflow of the one self-luminous awareness, which, being aware of itself, is free to be aware of all.

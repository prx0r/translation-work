**The self-luminous cognition is never the object of another**

We have seen that the yogin, by the purity and the un-opposed spreading of his cognition, knows
another's cognition. But this draws a sharp objection from the epistemologist. "Even granting that," he
says, "a cognition can never be the *object* of another cognition. For how can one awareness be known
by a second? The two would have to stand in the relation of object and holder — and between two
cognitions no such relation is possible."

Abhinavagupta takes this seriously and analyzes what "being an object" really means. A thing is an
object of a cognition when the awareness *assimilates its form* — when it takes on the form of the
thing, as the blue-awareness takes on the blue. This assimilation is the sārūpya, the conformity of the
cognition to its object. And it requires that the object be *both* like and unlike the cognition: in
total identity the thing would be the cognition itself, and in total difference there would be nothing
to assimilate. Now here is the crux. A cognition has no such difference from another cognition. The
difference of place, time, and form is absent between two awarenesses; the difference that comes from
the object (the blue) is not the param-essence of the cognition; and though the object-part — the blue
— or the intent-part — the passion — can be an object by a separate manifestation, the *awareness-part*
itself can never be an object at all. An awareness cannot be spread before itself as a thing to be
grasped.

Abhinavagupta makes this vivid with two mirrors. Two mirrors can reflect one another, each bearing the
other's image — because their forms, being of place and time, are genuinely distinct. But two
cognitions are not like that. Their form is not the place-time form of things; it is the self-luminous
awareness, which has nothing in it to be set over against another awareness. So the object-holder
relation between two cognitions is impossible: there is no perception of any real difference between
them (this is the epistemologist's own anupalabdhi, turned against him).

And what of māyā — does it not cover the awareness and make it an object? No. Māyā does not really
cover the awareness's own form, any more than a covering hides the burning power of the fire; and even
what we call a covering is, in truth, the producing of a form distinct from the prior state. The
awareness is not hidden by the world; it is the world that appears in the awareness.

The upshot is decisive. The cognition is self-luminous. It is never made the object of another
cognition — because to be an object is to be assimilated by an awareness, and one awareness cannot
assimilate another without the real difference that awarenesses lack. The knower, in other words, is
fundamentally *conscious* — the ajaḍa, the not-inert — a self-luminous presence that is never itself
turned into a thing. This is the establishment of the conscious knower, with which the vimarśa on
memory, and the whole Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi, comes to its close.

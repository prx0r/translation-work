**The knowledge-power: manifestation as the very form of things**

We have examined memory and the construction. But both presuppose something deeper — the
knowledge-power (jñānaśakti), the capacity by which anything at all is known. And here Abhinavagupta
makes the central claim of this vimarśa: **the manifestation is the very form of the objects.** The
things of the world are not hidden realities that a light shines upon from outside; their being *is*
their being-manifested. To know a thing is not to touch something beyond the light; it is to be the
light in which that thing appears.

Consider the present beings. As they manifest, they manifest "as the outer-self" — the inner
standing-alone of consciousness, pouring itself out as the external world. The jar before you is not a
thing outside that your mind then copies; it is the inner light, standing as the outer form. Memory and
the construction, for their part, do not present a common object as something separate and distinct; and
even in perception, the object does not exceed the experience that reveals it. The thing is not more
than its being-manifested.

Now the Vijñānavādin, the Buddhist idealist, agrees that the object is not outside — but he draws the
wrong conclusion. He concludes that there is no object at all, only the inner cognition with its blue
form. Abhinavagupta replies that this does not follow. The cognition's inner blue-form is a
*reflection*; and a reflection expects a ground that it reflects — as a mirror-image expects the thing
before the mirror. So the inner blue-form, being a reflection, points to an identical ground that
produces it. This is the inference of the object: not as a thing hidden beyond all experience, but as
the ground whose reflection the blue-form is. Even as the sense-organ is inferred from the perception
it causes, so the object is inferred as the cause of its manifestation. The object is real — but its
reality is to be the ground of the manifestation, not a bare thing beyond it.

And this is the deep point. The objects necessarily inhere in the consciousness-self, by the very
re-recollection (parāmarśa) through which they are known. And the essence of the manifestation — the
knowledge-power — is the reflexive-awareness (vimarśa). The light is not a passive mirror; it is the
self-aware light that, in knowing the object, knows itself. The known is not something set over against
the knower; the knowledge-re-recollection is itself the known, the pure, never abandoning the
knower-being. Even in the un-constructed perception, the life is the reflexive-awareness. So the
knowledge-power is not the capacity to reach some hidden thing; it is the self-luminous, self-aware
manifestation that is the very being of the world.

The upshot: the jñānaśakti is the manifestation that constitutes the objects. The world is not lit
from without; it is the light. The object is not a fiction, nor a thing beyond the light, but the ground
that the manifestation reflects; and the essence of that manifestation is the reflexive-awareness —
the light that knows itself in knowing all. This is the knowledge-power, the first and deepest of the
powers of consciousness.

**How the external object is inferred and confirmed**

The Vijñānavādin grants that the cognition is self-luminous, but he presses on its inner form. "The
cognition's blue-form," he says, "is a reflection (pratibimba); and a reflection demands a ground that
produces it (the arpaka). So the external object is inferred." But then the classic objection follows:
how can you infer an external object that is never perceived? An inference rests on a pervasion grasped
in perception — and the external is never perceived at all.

Abhinavagupta answers with the sense-organ analogy. The sense-organ itself is never perceived — yet we
infer it as the cause of the perceptions it produces. The eye is inferred from the seeing, not seen.
So too the external object: though never perceived as an object, it is inferred as the cause of its
cognition. And the mechanism is the sārūpya, the conformity: the inner blue-form, with all its
place-and-time variety, un-lost, points to its ground — the object similar to it. The determination "I
see this object" arises from this conformity, and it is not a bare error, like the shell-silver in the
grasped shell.

For here is the crucial point, and Abhinavagupta states it boldly: even this determination, though it
begins as error — the drawing of the inner form outward — is, by its bond to the thing, a *valid means*.
As the rule has it, "even the error, being a relation, is a pramā." The determination, by the strength
of the seeing, takes the form "I see" and enlists the perception as the object's helper — as a minister
moves a king who has been won over. So the external object, thus determined and confirmed, is
perceived (pratyakṣa-gṛhīta).

And what, then, is the inference's own work? It is the sasaṃvāda — the agreement. The inference
establishes that the root-cause, the ground of the forms (the arpaka), is the object similar to the
form. This is its operation: not to create the object, but to confirm that the ground of the cognition's
form is the thing, the similar thing, which the determination had already perceived.

And if the inference were to work the other way — to refute the external, as the reasoning from
momentariness refutes stability — then the whole determination of the external would be uprooted, and
the practical dealings of the world would fail. If it did not determine at all, the dealing would
likewise fail. So the external object, perceived and confirmed by the agreement, stands: it is real, and
the cognition of it, with the perception as its helper and the inference as its confirmation, is a
valid means.

The upshot is a balanced realism. The object is not a bare thing hidden beyond all cognition; it is the
ground of the cognition's form, inferred like the sense-organ and confirmed by the agreement. The
determination "I see the jar" is not a fiction — it is a valid means, because it is bound to the thing
and confirmed by the agreement. The external object is real, and the way we know it — perception and
inference together — is sound.

**You cannot infer another's mind — and you do not need the external object**

The Buddhist hopes to escape by inferring *another's* mind. He thinks: from Maitra's speech, from his
uttered words and gestures, one can infer what is going on in his mind — the other stream. Abhinavagupta
shows this inference cannot work, and he does it with a precise analogy.

Suppose you want to infer the fire *in the sandalwood* from the smoke. If you set aside the
sandalwood's fragrance — the very thing that marks this smoke as coming from sandalwood — then you
establish only fire in general, not the sandalwood-fire. The distinction must be accepted; without it
the desired conclusion is not reached. But to infer another's mind, you would need exactly such a
further distinction — the "being-gone-to-the-other," the fact that this utterance belongs to Maitra and
not to you. And that distinction cannot be inferred from the mere utterance. The utterance could belong
to anything; nothing in it, by itself, says "this is Maitra's intent, this is another's mind."

And the "being-gone-to-the-other," Abhinavagupta argues, is not like the sandalwood-fragrance, a
further distinguishing mark resting apart from the thing. It is a different kind of thing entirely. To
press the point, he notes that if every additional feature were a "further distinction," then "cow" and
"speckled" would both be separate things, and the statement "the cow is the speckled" would fail. But
that statement holds; so a real further-distinction is something that rests apart from the thing, and
the "being-gone-to-the-other" does not rest apart from the mere utterance in the way that would make it
inferable.

Moreover — and this is the decisive point — only the *aimed* action (samīhitā) is the wish's effect.
A wish produces the action it intends, not a mere utterance or gesture that could be aimed at anything.
So from Maitra's mere utterance one cannot infer his wish, since the utterance does not reveal which
aim it serves. If the mere utterance sufficed, then even the most grotesque case — a piśāca-like action
in someone's appearing — would have to be established as his intent, which is absurd.

So the other-stream cannot be inferred; the mere appearances do not reach another's mind. And this
paves the way for kārikā 6: the appearances alone suffice for the dealings of the world. We do not need
a hidden external object behind the appearances to explain our dealing with the jar, the fire, the
person. The appearance — the ābhāsa — is enough. And from this follows the refutation of the external
object: whether it is posited as a whole (the avayavin of the Nyāya) or as atoms (the paramāṇu of the
Vaibhāṣika), the external thing is needless. Only the appearance is real, and it is the appearance that
serves our object-action (arthakriyā). The world of our dealing is the world of the appearances — and
these, being the manifestation of consciousness, are real.

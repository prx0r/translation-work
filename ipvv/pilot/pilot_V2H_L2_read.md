**The light that knows itself: the heart of the doctrine**

Here, in the deepest part of the vimarśa on the knowledge-power, Abhinavagupta states the heart of the
whole teaching. The manifestation (prakāśa) is not a passive, inert light. Its very nature is the
*reflexive-awareness* (vimarśa) — the light's own grasp of itself. Without the vimarśa, the
manifestation would be like crystal: it would show the world, but it would not know that it showed it;
it would be inert. What makes the light *light* — what makes it consciousness and not a thing — is that
it is aware of itself in the very act of manifesting.

Consider the very first surge of knowing. In the inner speech, the middle-self, before the mind has
split the knower from the known — in the paśyantī, the seeing, the first stirring of the will — the
knower and the known are not yet two, not even by their common form; no distinction has yet flashed.
And this, Abhinavagupta says, shines by itself. It is the pure reflexive-awareness before any division.

The proof that this is so is drawn from the swiftest of our acts. In quick reading, in rapid motion,
in hurried speech, we go from line to line, from place to place, abandoning some things and seizing
others — all without pausing to reflect. Yet these abandonments and attainments are, as we see, always
preceded by a re-reflection (parāmarśa). They infer their cause, the re-reflection. And that
re-reflection cannot be only by the difference of knower and known — for then the very swiftness of the
act would fail, tangled in the lineage of speech, mind, motion, convention, memory. The swift act shows
that the knower and the known, the abandoning and the attaining, are not two separate things: they are
one act of the self-aware consciousness. What is, and is not separate, must be non-separate — there is
no third class to hold them apart.

And this is the will — the first surge, the first "tuṭi" (the bursting). As the Śivadṛṣṭi says: when
the consciousness-dharma, in its splendor and joy, unfolds into the varied creation, and becomes the
un-lidded awareness — that is the will's first surge. And she is seen in the sudden moments: in the
heart, at the time of remembering an effect, at the moment of rapture or emotion, at the sight of the
beloved, at the un-reflected glance, at the very emission of the utterance, in the reading and the
running. In all these occasions, the all-power-liveliness of the consciousness flashes. It is the
reflexive-awareness, the self-grasping of the light, seen most clearly where the mind does not pause to
divide.

So the reflexive-awareness is not a thing, not an added quality — it is the manifestation's own nature.
The light that shines is the light that knows itself; and this self-knowing is the parā-vāk, the
supreme speech, from which all the lower levels of speech (the madhyamā, the vaikharī) descend. It is
the Lord's freedom (svātantrya) and his lordship (aiśvarya). This is what Abhinavagupta will draw out
in the next kārikās — that consciousness is at once the awareness, its action, and its agency (kārikā
12), and that this reflexive-awareness, the parā-vāk, arising by its own savor, is the supreme freedom
of the supreme self (kārikā 13). The heart of the doctrine is here: the light knows itself, and that
self-knowing is the Lord.

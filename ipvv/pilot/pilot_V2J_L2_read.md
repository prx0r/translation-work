**Memory's seed and the "this jar" are the reflexive-awareness**

The reflexive-awareness is the source of the powers. Now Abhinavagupta shows that even the *impression*
— the seed of memory — is nothing without it. How does a memory get laid down, and how does it awaken?
The schools would say: the experience lays a trace, and the later seeing of something like it awakens
the trace. But Abhinavagupta presses: what makes the experience strong enough to lay the seed? It is
the resting of the experience in the reflexive-awareness — the moment in which the experience is aware
of itself, "I, thus, shine." Without that self-awareness, the experience would be an inert flash; it
could not lay a seed, nor could it be awakened. The non-construction, the bare sensory flash, cannot do
it.

The Bhaṭṭa had said the strength of the experience is what lays the seed. But even this strength,
Abhinavagupta replies, is the resting in the knower — the reflexive-awareness. If the light's
manifestation, its being-strong, is not admitted, then nothing at all would lay a seed; and the
not-strong would have to be the awakener too, making the whole "strong" distinction meaningless. The
point is driven home with the smoke-and-fire example. "Fire-ness itself is the smoke-cause-ness" — we
see this in the fire. If the mere impression, without the vimarśa, laid the memory, then even the
coconut-island dweller, who has never seen smoke, would remember fire from smoke. But he does not.
Without the vimarśa-form cause, the impression is neither laid nor awakened — just as the smoke does
not awaken the fire-memory in one who never knew fire.

And this is the deeper point: the very act by which we take the world — the determination
(adhyavasāya) — is itself the reflexive-awareness. When we say "this jar," the determination "this
jar" is not a mere name-and-form, a word pasted onto a thing. It is, Abhinavagupta declares in kārikā
20, *beyond* name and form: it is the param-Lord's power, manifesting like the self, not by the
this-ness. The "this" of the jar is not something the jar has from outside; it is the Lord's own
reflexive-awareness, shining through the act of determination. The vācya-vācaka (the signified and the
signifier), the sphoṭa — all these analyses fail, because the "this-ness" they try to locate in the
word or the thing is really the self's own manifestation.

So memory and determination are both the reflexive-awareness. The impression is laid and awakened only
by the vimarśa; and the determination "this jar," far from being a word pasted on a thing, is the
param-Lord's power manifesting like the self. The vimarśa is not one power among others; it is the
very form of the light, present in the memory's seed, in the determination's "this," and in every act
by which the self knows and takes its world.

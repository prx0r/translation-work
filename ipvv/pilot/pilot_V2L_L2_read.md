**The "I" is not a construction**

We have established knowledge and memory. Now Abhinavagupta takes up the third power — the apohana, the
removal — that serves both. And at its heart lies a question that threatens the whole doctrine. The
reflexive-awareness, we have said, is the manifestation's own nature. But the reflexive-awareness is
word-joined; and the word-joined, it might seem, is a construction (vikalpa). If so, then the Lord's own
self-awareness would be a construction — which cannot be. The construction is of the māyā-form, the
saṃsāric; how could it be in the Lord?

Kārikā 1 answers this, and its answer is precise. The "I"-recollection (ahaṃ-pratyavamarśa) — even
though it has the word as its body — is *not* a construction. It is the fit, the two-throwing, the
determination.

Consider what the pure "I"-recollection is. It is the self-grasp of the manifestation free of the
contraction by the body and the rest — the grasp of the self-nature, resting in the freedom, supreme,
foundational: the "I." This, Abhinavagupta insists, is not a construction. And to see why, we must ask
what a construction actually is. The construction (vikalpa, the vivida-kalpanā) is of three kinds: it
joins what is distinct (as one joins molasses, curd, and pepper into a compound); it constructs the one
in many ways; or it splits off a doubted, other form. All three are operations of division and joining,
of this-and-that.

But the "I"-recollection is none of these. It does not join a distinct "this" to a distinct "I"; it is
the single self-grasp, resting in itself. It does not construct the one into many; it is the one.
It does not split off a doubted other; it is the un-doubted foundation. The "I" is not built up out of
parts, nor split out of a doubt — it is the pure self-awareness that underlies every construction, the
very thing that makes construction possible, without itself being one.

This is the apohanaśakti at its root. The removal — the power by which the self excludes what is not
itself — is not a construction that the self performs upon itself; it is the self's own grasp, the pure
"I" that, in knowing itself, is the foundation of all the word-joined, constructed dealings of the
world. And this pure "I"-recollection, resting in the freedom, is the Lord's self-awareness — not a
māyā-form construction, but the very nature of the manifestation, the self-grasp that is the Lord.
The apohana, the removal, is not a cutting off; it is the self's own luminous grasp, the "I" that is
one, un-divided, and free.

**The omniscient knows all — and his non-perception establishes**

The saptamo vimarśa, on the one support of the powers, closes with the figure of the omniscient — the
supreme knower who is the support. And the question that tests him is the goblin, the piśāca. Suppose
there is a piśāca in the dark interval. We do not see it; but the atoms of form and the rest are there,
unseen by us, seen by the omniscient. The piśāca's atoms, for the omniscient, are no different from the
atoms of the darkness and the other beings — he sees them all. If the piśāca is truly absent, then where
it might have stood, the omniscient sees only the atoms of light (āloka), and these — being seen — are
the very ground of his dealing "there is no piśāca here."

But the objector presses: "seeing all the other objects, how does the omniscient deny a *distinct*
piśāca?" The answer is subtle. Whatever is, the omniscient sees it; so if, in that place, he does not
see the piśāca, then it is not there. Everything in that place is the piśāca-included thing — the whole
known together — and its being or not-being is decided by his seeing. The distinct-piśāca's-absence is
established by the omniscient's whole, un-broken perception, not by any tracing of the interval's
atoms.

And why is this not so for us? Because we see only the limited (arvāk). For one who sees the limited,
there is no such un-broken knowledge: his non-perception of the piśāca is only the limited — the
grasped-as-if-un-grasped — not the established non-being. There is no cause, in his limited seeing, to
remove the conceiving of the distinct thing in that place. The omniscient's non-perception, by contrast,
establishes; the limited seer's does not.

The final objection: "if the piśāca's non-negatability is caused by its invisibility, then the jar's
negatability should be caused by its visibility." But this fails: every negation, Abhinavagupta says,
culminates in the identity-negation — and there the visibility is un-deviating. The jar's negation
rests on its identity being negated in that place, and the seeing that grounds it is whole. The
omniscient's seeing is not the limited seeing of the ordinary; it is the whole, un-broken perception of
the one who knows all.

So the vimarśa closes on the omniscient as the support of the powers. He sees all the atoms, all the
beings, all the interval — and his perception, being whole, establishes what is and what is not. The
limited seer, the arvāgdarśana, sees only a part; the omniscient sees the whole. And this omniscient —
the one whose perception is un-broken, whose non-perception establishes, whose support is all the
powers — is the maheśvara, the one knower in whom all the powers rest. With his establishment, the
seventh vimarśa — the reflexion on the one support of the powers — comes to its close.

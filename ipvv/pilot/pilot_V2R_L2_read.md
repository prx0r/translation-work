**The great-Lordship: the freedom in the appearance-group**

The knower, with his powers of knowledge, memory, and removal, the inner-made-infinite-world-form, has
been established. Now Abhinavagupta asks what makes him *maheśvara*, the great Lord. The great-Lordship
(māheśvarya) is twofold: it is directed at objects of knowledge and at objects of action. Here, in the
jñānādhikāra, the knowledge-directed lordship is determined. What is the jñāna? It is the
self-experience "aham" — the "I" — contracted by the tint of the self-splitting appearance. And the
lordship is the *freedom in the appearance-group*: the freedom with which the appearances arise, vary,
and depend on one another.

The doubt arises at once. "All the dealing," the objector says, "is in the param-eśvara alone. But the
dealing's animator is one — there is nothing of its own beyond it. So what cause is there for the
varied dealing of the world, with all its clearness and variety?" And kārikā 1 answers: the appearances
are not all alike. Some are dependent on the eye's co-presence at that time — the "this jar," raised by
the impression's strength, depending on the perception that is near, sensory — and these appear in the
construction-dealing that follows immediately upon the perception. Others appear otherwise — in the
blind, in the darkness, and the rest, where there is no such eye-co-presence. The appearances are not
a single flat mass; they have their own dependence, their own conditions, their own arising.

And this is precisely the freedom. The varied dealing of the world is not caused by something outside
the Lord; it is the Lord's own freedom in the appearance-group — the freedom by which some appearances
depend on the senses, some do not, yet all are one. The appearance-group is not inert; it is the
manifestation of the Lord's freedom, the jñāna as the "aham" that shines through all the appearances,
unified in the one knower.

So the aṣṭamo vimarśa opens the great-Lordship. The Lord is maheśvara because he is free in the
appearance-group: the appearances arise, depend, and vary by his freedom, and yet they are one, because
they are all his own "aham"-manifestation. The knowledge-directed lordship is this freedom — the
self-experience that runs through all the appearances, the one "I" in which the whole world of
appearances rests. And this, Abhinavagupta will go on to show, grounds both the knowledge and the
action — the full great-Lordship of the one who is free in all the appearances, the maheśvara.

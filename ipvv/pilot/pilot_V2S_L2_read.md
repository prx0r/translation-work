**The knowers are one, and the one is the Lord**

The jñānādhikāra — the whole first adhikāra of the Īśvarapratyabhijñāvivṛtivimarśinī — comes to its
close. And it closes, fittingly, on the unity. It was said at the start that the one is the maheśvara;
now the unity is established. Kārikā 10: without that unity, the world-path of the consciousnesses
would not be; from the manifestation-unity, that one-ness, that one alone is the knower, it is
established.

The argument is simple and decisive. The whole dealing of the world — the path by which consciousnesses
know, remember, and act together — would be impossible if the consciousnesses were a scattered crowd of
mutually opaque streams. But the dealing is; so the consciousnesses must be one. And this is not hard to
accept, for the manifestation (prakāśa) is the very essence of the consciousnesses, and the
manifestation is one — there is no difference in the light itself. That one manifestation is, in truth,
the knower. The one light that shines in all the consciousnesses is the one knower, the param-essence
knower, the paramātman. Even the Sāṃkhya's and the Jaiminīya's self-luminous consciousness, and the
ancient Vaiśeṣika's consciousness-nature self, all point to this one.

The objection comes: "each cognition rests, in its own self-experience, in its own joining — what is
new here?" And the answer is that the known itself — the blue and the rest — has the manifestation as
its param-essence; the blue is, in its being, the light. So the unity is real, not a construction. And
the apparent difference of the known is made only by the māyā-power: the known-marker part, made
distinct from the manifestation as if it were other, is still the manifestation's own part. The
difference is the māyā's making; the unity is the truth.

And the Sāṃkhya's puruṣa — the separate self it posits — is not another at all. It is the un-recognized
own-lordship, the contracted consciousness; its knowledge and action, dealt as "I," are the
"I"-recollection. And this "I"-recollection, Abhinavagupta notes, is the pervader of all the states —
the pure, the mixed, the un-pure — gathered, not abandoned. The threefold self-recognition runs through
them all.

Then kārikā 11 sums up the whole: he alone, by the fixed reflexive-awareness-ness, is the great Lord;
the reflexive-awareness alone, of the God, in the pure knowledge-action, since. The great-Lordship is
nothing but the reflexive-awareness — the one who is aware of himself, fixedly, is the Lord; and that
reflexive-awareness is the God's own pure knowledge-and-action. The great-Lordship is not an added
power; it is the self-awareness itself, the "I" that knows and acts, the one knower.

And so the jñānādhikāra — the adhikāra on knowledge — is complete. The ||8|| colophon closes the
vimarśa on the great-Lordship in the one-support of the powers; and the great colophon reads:
"completed — this, the first jñānādhikāra." The first of the four adhikāras of the IPVV is done. It
has established the self, the powers, the one knower, the great-Lordship — all culminating in the
unity: the one manifestation is the one knower, and the one knower, aware of himself, is the Lord.

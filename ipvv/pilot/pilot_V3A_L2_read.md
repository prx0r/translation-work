**Action, time and sequence: the timeless that manifests order**

Having established the nature of the power of knowledge, Abhinavagupta now turns to the power of
*action*. The two must run in parallel: if consciousness's power to know the world has been shown,
then its power to *effect* the world must be shown too. And here he must confront a paradox that sits
at the very heart of his system. The Lord is beyond time and sequence — utterly free of order — and
yet the world he manifests is shot through with sequence: causes before effects, moments before
moments, place before place. How can the timeless act in time?

He announces the answer in the opening verse of homage. He bows to Śiva, he says, "who, though free
of sequence, manifests manifold sequences." The whole doctrine is in that one image. And the image he
will use to make it work is the mirror. A mirror bears countless reflections — of things before it,
beside it, in all their variety — without ever ceasing to be the one, undivided mirror. It is not
torn into pieces by what it shows. So too the one consciousness, whose essence is nothing but
appearance, can take up within itself a host of seemingly contradictory forms — the ordered, distinct
appearances of the world — without its own oneness being divided in the least. This is the
"action-support" (kriyāśraya): the one that acts is one, and it is precisely *because* it is one and
un-divided that it can show sequence at all.

This is aimed squarely at the Buddhist. The Buddhist had said: only momentary cognitions flash
forth, in a stream, with no owner behind them; memory is nothing but impression; and cognition is
either inert — in which case it cannot reveal an object — or conscious, in which case it has no real
temporal distinctions, so there is no self that is anything other than this flux. Abhinavagupta
answers each point. Distinct cognitions cannot serve the ordinary dealings of the world — a string of
unrelated flashes could never get anyone to the village. Memory is not mere impression. And cognition
is the *un-contracted* consciousness, contracting itself by its own power under the tint of the
known object — and that self-contraction is what we call a "power" and a "dharma." It is one, it is
conscious, and it is not inert; and its oneness is no more threatened by its many appearances than a
mirror's is by its reflections.

So the first objection — "the action of the one is not ordered" — is rejected, precisely by
establishing that *one* as the agent. The one can be of many natures; that is not a contradiction.

But now a sharper objection. "Order is essential to action," the Buddhist says, "and how could order
be in the Lord, who is not cut through by time?" Here Abhinavagupta draws the crucial distinction. It
is the *worldly* action that is ordered. Consider the raising and lowering of a hand. Its earlier and
later moments are ordered; the single action that is the cause of the hand's moving from one position
to another — even if in itself eternal — is *inferred* as ordered, by the force of its ordered effect.
So the action of ordinary worldly dealing rightly rests in distinctions and is rightly called
ordered. What makes it so is the **time-power** (kālaśakti). And this time-power is nothing alien:
it is simply the Lord's own freedom, the freedom that raises the distinctions of appearance and lets
one thing follow another.

But the *Lord's own* action is a different matter. Because the Lord is not cut through by time, his
action is not cut through by time either: it is eternal, like the Lord himself. In the manifesting of
the known and the knower that underlies all our knowledge and dealing, the knowledge-power is the
capacity to reveal; but the reflexive-awareness-power — whose very self is freedom in joining and
separating — is, in truth, the action. And this action, though in reality free of all sequence,
*appears* ordered: it takes up the delimited forms of things in order to reveal the known, as if
casting the shadow of the known before it. It is the timeless showing time, the mirror showing order
while remaining one.

And so the paradox dissolves. The worldly action is ordered, by the time-power — which is the Lord's
freedom. The Lord's own action is eternal, un-cut by time. And the two are not in conflict, because
the sequence we see is precisely the Lord's timeless freedom manifesting itself as order — the mirror
showing many, while remaining one. This is the foundation on which the rest of the vimarśa — the
nature of time, the order of place, and finally the creation-power — will build.

## What time is (kārikā 3)

What, then, is time? People point to the sun's course, the flowering of the seasons, heat and cold.
But in truth — Abhinavagupta insists — time is *order itself*. The Vaiśeṣika posits a time-substance,
the Sāṃkhya identifies it with rajas, the grammarians with an eternal, the Buddhist with the stream of
momentary being; but all of them, in the end, are describing the same thing: a *sequence*, a this-after-
that, projected outward and called "time." 

Consider how we actually measure. We measure gold by a fixed weight; we measure the day by the sun's
apparent course — "Caitra walks for a day." And the sun's own motion, in turn, is measured by the
water-clock. In every case, the "time" is nothing but the *fixed difference of nature* of some
appearance — the sun, the heat, the cuckoo — appearing as a measured sequence. And the power that makes
that sequence *appear*, that arranges appearances one after another, is the Lord's own power. Time is
therefore not a substance that cuts through things; it is order entering into appearance — the very
freedom of consciousness showing its appearances as before-and-after, as a dream or a magician's show
is arranged by the one who dreams it.

## Order rests on what appears and what does not (kārikā 4)

But is time merely *difference*? No — for then even the fingers, which differ, would have temporal
difference. What grounds order is deeper: it is the *being and not-being of appearances* — what is
manifest and what is not. Order is the difference between the spring that appears and the summer whose
appearance is withheld. And who withholds and reveals? The Lord, by his own *freedom* (svātantrya). He
manifests his appearances in every relation — the jar on the firm iron, the jar and the cloth as
distinct, or the two as one undivided juice — and it is only when he reveals the spring as empty of the
summer that order "blinks" into being. This power to manifest and to withhold is the **time-power**
(kālaśakti). So order, and time with it, is not a thing that imposes itself on the Lord; it is the
Lord's freedom, showing appearances as one-then-another. He is the stage-director of the world-machine,
who binds and releases, and so divides the universe into before and after.

## Place-order and time-order (kārikā 5)

The same freedom accounts for *place* as well as time. The variety of formed things — house, courtyard,
shop, shrine, garden, forest — gives rise to place-order, the extension and separation of things in
space. But place-order belongs to *many* things. Consider, by contrast, the ripening fruit: it is one
thing, yet it ripens — it passes through states one after another. That is time-order, and it belongs
to the *one*. The Lord, by his freedom, manifests the variety of actions — "Caitra goes," "the fruit
ripens" — and so brings time-order into being. And note where time lives: in the *known*, not in
consciousness. Even in a cognition, time touches only the known-part, the object as it appears — never
the self-awareness, the pure act of knowing, which is free of it.

## The finite knower, not the Lord, is bound by time (kārikā 6)

Here is the crucial turn. Order — the being and not-being of appearances — belongs to the *finite*
knower, the one whose marker is the void-thought, the breath, the body. For his appearance is
*discontinuous* (vicchinna): his shining is not his own form; he is, as it were, inert, and it is only
consciousness flashing *as* him that lets him appear at all. Such a knower is always passing in and
out of manifestation, and so he is always in time. But the supreme knower — the one "once-shown"
(sakṛt), whose manifestation is continuous and un-broken — never has his appearances appear and
disappear. For him there is no before and after. Time does not touch him; and, since it is only with
reference to him that beings are in time, in the end time does not touch the beings either.

## Place-order too is for the measured, not the immeasurable (kārikā 7)

And the same must be said of place. The beings of the finite, *measured* knower shine as occupying
mutually exclusive places — this jar here, that body there. That is the finite knower's experience of
place. But the *immeasurable* knower — whose very self is manifestation, full by itself — has no such
division. His beings shine *in him*, like himself, un-measured, free of place-order. So with respect to
the pure self, the Lord has no place-order; and, by reference to him, neither do the beings. Time and
place, the two great frameworks of the world, are both, in the end, nothing but the Lord's freedom —
and they bind only the measured, the finite, the discontinuous; never the immeasurable, the
once-shown, the eternal.

## The closing (kārikās 8–9)

The world, Abhinavagupta sums up, is the consciousness-dense self-filled — and this, the whole account
of time and order, is the statement of that self-fullness. And with this he turns to what comes next:
the *creation-power* (nirmāṇaśakti) of the thus-knowing Lord — the power by which the one who knows all
this actually creates the world. The foundation is laid: the Lord is one, free, timeless and placeless;
and time and place are but his freedom showing the world as order, binding only the measured and the
finite.

**Number and measure: relational, not real**

Every real entity we can speak of comes numbered. "No real thing is expressed without a number" — a
maxim Abhinavagupta takes seriously. But he turns it to his own end. For number is not an extra thing
that attaches to the object like a property; it is the very structure of *saying* a thing at all. In
any numbered expression, the number takes the subordinate place. It is the nature of a case-ending to
be a mere qualification of the nominal meaning. So when we say "one" or "two," what we grasp as
primary is not a number-thing floating free, but the *mutual delimitation* of the things — each
appearing as cutting off and being cut off by the others. The object's own form, then, is what comes
numbered; and "number" itself is just *the counting of distinctions*, not a reality added to the
object.

The same is true of *measure*. The Kāṇādas are famous for their measure — the atom, the great, the
short, the long. Take the short and the long first. Length is a measure: it fits the definition. But
what is "measure" in the first place? It is *relative to a delimited other thing*. It is the
*appearance of comparison*: the familiar cubit of the hand — twenty-four digits — against which we
compare the cloth to be measured. And crucially, this is not a mere external contact with the hand; it
is the *act of comparing*. Measure, then, is not a magnitude lying in the thing itself; it is the
appearance of the thing as measured *against a standard*. It is precisely to raise the distinction of
short and long that we take up the measuring-rod at all.

Now the Kāṇādas also speak of the "atom" and the "great." What could these be? Atom-ness is not mere
part-less-ness — for then it would belong to the part-less qualities and actions. Nor is it the
substance's being-part-less — for then space itself would be an atom. Both fail. Atom-ness is instead
a *measure of delimitation*: it is the fitness of a thing to be cut off from all sides, like the very
subtle thing that is the lightest of the mustard-seed, the pod, the small box, the little box. That
alone is the atom; its opposite is the great. Atom and great are not absolute natures — they are ways
of being delimited, relative to other things.

And so it is with all three of the Kāṇāda measures. There is the measure "from all sides" — the
volume-measure, as of sesame measured by a wooden cup, whose mark is again the appearance of
comparison, not mere contact. There is the length-measure, the comparison against the hand or rod.
And there is the *upward* measure — weight — which the Kāṇādas identify with heaviness causing the
fall of a thing. But here too Abhinavagupta corrects them. In mere heaviness there is no act of
measuring; what we weigh against the balance-weights is the *comparison* of heavinesses, and that
comparison alone is the weight-measure. Measure-ness is thus one and the same in all three cases —
differing only in whether the measuring runs "extended," "around," or "upward." So, in buying and
selling, gold is weighed by the balance, and its measure is simply the comparison-appearance of
heaviness; we use it, as if by an inner unity-part, by the greater and the lesser alone.

Even *separateness* is the same: it is preceded by the grasping of distinctions, and it is the
appearance of difference whose middle is the un-difference internally conjectured.

So the Vaiśeṣika's reification of number and measure — as real features of substances — dissolves.
Number is counting; measure is comparison; both are *relational appearances* of a thing delimiting and
being delimited by others. This is the distinction-and-non-distinction doctrine, applied to quantity:
the substance-cognition, like the action-cognition before it, is true not because it grasps a real
intrinsic magnitude, but because it is stable and efficacious in the dealings of the world.

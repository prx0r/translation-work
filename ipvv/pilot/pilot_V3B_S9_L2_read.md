**The conventional-true: not a fiction, but anchored in the world's usage**

The opponent had tried to collapse the conventional into the merely fictional. "The convention-mind
is a coverer," he said. "It attributes to an object what was never actually seen — such as the
universal. A real, actually-seen thing is never attributed to a universal in this way." 

Abhinavagupta's reply is that this does not even distinguish the case. Take the double-moon. Its
"twoness" is, after all, *seen* — but the very same seen-ness belongs equally to any one of many
objects. So by this criterion, delusion-ness acquires no special character that the conventional
lacks. And more: even calling the conventional a *species of* delusion is not something the text-
author accepts. He names "convention and delusion" as a *coordinate pair* — a dvandva. And a dvandva
joins things of equal form. The point is that the conventional is not a sub-species of the illusory
to be filed under it; it stands on its own, coordinate with the real.

But what, positively, is the universal in this scheme? It is not a bare, free-floating form. The
universal, without swallowing up all the specifics, works amid them; it takes up one specific as its
intended object and bends it toward itself, never obtaining a word-entry of its own apart from those
specifics. The universal lives *in* the individuals, not apart from them.

The proof is direct, drawn from the plain usage of the world. In ordinary Ārya speech, the words of
the "dāntava" and "līvarda" kind are what people use to point at *bulls* — the male of the class —
whereas the single word "cow" refers, in that same usage, to the female cattle alone. So the word
"cow" does not float free as a bare universal: it is anchored, by the world's own practice, to a
specific sub-class of the animals. And this is exactly how the cognition runs. When someone says
"this city has cows," what flashes in the mind is simply the city's abundance of milk and curds — not
a detached "cow-ness," and not any claim about the animals' sex. The meaning of the conventional term
is its practical upshot: the milk and the curds it leads you to expect.

This gives the marker of the conventional: it is *this much, and no more*. And that much even the
opponent, however unwilling, must concede. For the point is decisive. If you remove both the
*manifestation* and the *use* — the two tests by which we distinguished the true from the false — then
the conventional collapses into impossibility, and our position stands. If, on the other hand, you
insist that the use fails, then it is the *opponent's* sense of use that dissolves. On either horn,
the conventional-true holds its ground: it is a genuine, world-anchored entity — coordinate with the
real, never a mere fiction — whose entire being is the practical tracking of a sub-class of things.

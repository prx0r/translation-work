**How many appearances make one thing**

Abhinavagupta had concluded that the valid means operates *on the appearance alone*. But this raises
a pressing problem. The world treats things as *one* — the jar, the fire, the gold. If the means seizes
only an appearance, how can it ever reach the single thing those appearances belong to? And how can
many, ever-shifting appearances all belong to one and the same thing? The answer, developed across six
kārikās, turns on the *synthesizing awareness* — the one reflective grasp that gathers the many
appearances into one.

Take the jar. At first glance it is simply one. But reflect: the discriminating mind divides it. It
divides it according to our freedom, according to our wish, according to the conventions we have
learned. Looked at from one side it is long, from another round, from another upright; it is a being,
a substance, a jar, gold, a shining thing. So the one jar shows many appearances. What, then, keeps it
one? It is the *synthesis* (anusandhāna): the reflexive-awareness that is the very light by which all
these appearances shine. Sometimes this awareness rests in the single appearance and yields the
universal — the "jar-ness" that is the same in every jar; sometimes it mixes "this, of-this-form" and
is the synthesis that joins the appearances into the one thing. It is by the synthesis that the many
appearances of the jar are established as one jar.

The appearances, though divided by viewpoint, wish, and trained perception — the smoke and sandalwood
are known by a perception born of practice — are nevertheless *un-divided by place and time*. They all
belong here and now. So the divisions are not of the thing's being somewhere at some time; they are
divisions of how it appears, gathered by the one awareness.

Now the subtlety of the object-action. When appearances mix, one of them is primary and is the common
locus (sāmānādhikaraṇya) of the others. Quickened by the sentence-awareness "this here, now, is the
jar," the appearances that have attained one own-marker-ness — without abandoning their difference —
do their object-actions. Some actions belong to the aggregate; some are fixed, each to each, singly.
The important point: it is the *object-action* that makes a thing real in the world's sense. And many
appearances can do *one* object-action. Here is the image Abhinavagupta gives. Separate lamp-lights do
not, of themselves, light up a single dwelling; but gathered in one house, they do one thing — they
make it bright. Streams, falling separately into the ocean with their countless waves, nevertheless as
a whole do one thing. So the jar, the gold, the red, the great — their several appearances together
produce a single object-action, say the great delight fit for bathing the image of Śiva. And the doer
of that one effect must be one. The one cognition, the one reflexive-awareness, is marked by the one
object-action.

And what about the fire — its heat, its denotation as "fire"? Here Abhinavagupta draws the crucial
distinction between what needs nothing else and what depends on another. The heat-appearance of the
fire is *un-opposed, un-created, fixed everywhere and always* — it needs no other object. But the
fire-as-fuel, the fire-as-cause-of-smoke — these *depend on another object* (the fuel, the smoke).
And the very meaning of the word "fire" is fixed by convention. All of these — the heat, the denotation
— are the fire's own-form (svabhāva), and they are established by the *one* valid means: the single
perception that looks, as it were, to the glance of the determinative power (niyati). So even though
the means seizes the appearance, the thing's own-form — what makes the fire fire and not something
else — is fixed, and known through the one perception.

The conclusion of the whole sequence: the pramāṇa operates on appearance, but it does not lose the
thing. The one synthesizing awareness gathers the many appearances into the one thing; the one
object-action confirms its reality; and the niyati-power fixes its own-form. The one and the many are
not in conflict — they are two phases of the same appearance, gathered by the awareness and fixed by
the power. This is the distinction-and-non-distinction doctrine, applied to the valid means itself.

**Grace, initiation, and the one liberated while living**

All that has been established — the valid means, its fruit, the primacy of the knower — now comes to
its practical point. How does one actually attain the Lord? The whole vimarśa closes with the answer:
by the Lord's grace, working through a fourfold effort, and culminating in the one liberated while
still living.

There is a supreme work to be accomplished. Even if it seems impossible in itself, it is undertaken
as possible; and the effort that drives it is the life of the fourfold work. But the decisive factor is
not the effort alone — it is the *grace* of the Lord. Only when the Lord's self is pleased, when the
turbidity of our conceit in the body and the rest is abandoned, are we able to cross the great ocean
of delusion. The means are laid out in the Lord-taught teaching: the initiatory rite (dīkṣā), the
vision-yoga (dhyāna), knowledge (jñāna), and devotion (bhāvanā). By their combined capacity, the
aspirant abandons the world that is to be left behind — the knower-made world of body, breath, the
eight-fold city and the void, the world of the things that support and objectify enjoyment — and,
through the grace of the Lord and the guru, brings the lordship itself to manifestation within his own
self.

The order matters. Although vision and knowledge are primary, knowledge is preceded by initiation. As
the teaching says, there is no eligibility for the yoga of Śaṅkara without the dīkṣā; and the dīkṣā is
twofold, by rite and by knowledge. But what if one, assured by the knowledge-initiation alone, has not
practiced the vision-yoga? Then there is devotion (bhāvanā). Some, the yogins, attain a share of the
wondrous powers — a portion of all-knowing and all-doing, the eight-fold vibhūti, pertaining to this
or that world. But even where such powers are manifest, nothing of the present goal is lost; and this,
too, is established by reason.

Now a subtle point about the self. The prāṇa — the vital breath, the body, the rest — is experienced
as "I." How is this compatible with the param-ātman, the supreme self? There is no conflict, because
the two are present together. The prāṇa's being felt as "I" is nothing other than the supreme self's
own free manifestation: the param-ātman shines freely, and by that very shining the prāṇa and the
rest appear as "I." Even the knower-ness that touches them is that same light. The object is
remembered, because experience is broken and re-collected; but the knower — whose essence is the
un-broken light — is not something that could be remembered. As the Spandakārikā says, remembered-ness
is not how the essence is realized. So the Lord-ness is not a new thing to be proved in the
consciousness-essence; and the negation of "I" in the breath and the body is not a new discovery
either. Both were there from the beginning.

And so the end. In all this there is the utter extinction of the delusion-impression. And when that
is extinguished, when the restraint of the body-and-"I" conceit dissolves, there is the Lord-ness
itself. Yet even here, by the Lord's will alone, for some the recognition does not arise despite a
hundred efforts. But even for such a one, merely by coming to hear the teaching, by the ordered
ripening of the impression, there is necessarily the attainment of one's own form. The āgama declares:
"Unwavering in self-experience, he is called the jīvanmukta, the one liberated while living; but he
who still constructs the essence — at the fall of the body, he goes to Śiva." And Abhinavagupta adds
his own verse: the sparks of the pure-knowledge-fire blaze when the inner intent is ended, like the
root-leaves of the old tree of saṃsāra caught in the fire of the supreme reality.

Hence, by all this, **nothing new is established in the self**. The Lord was always the own self;
grace, initiation, vision, knowledge and devotion only reveal what was already there. This, Abhinavagupta
says, is what was declared at the very beginning of the teaching — in the agent, the knower, the own
self, the ādi-siddha, the already-established. The tṛtīyo vimarśa — the reflexive-awareness of the
valid means, its fruit, and the supreme truth of the known — here comes to its close.

- **NOT resolved:** the canonical enumeration of the fourfold kṛtya; the exact ontology of the

**Verdict:** understood and readable. The tṛtīyo vimarśa closes cleanly: attainment is by grace
through the fourfold effort (initiation first), the prāṇa's "I" is the supreme self's free
manifestation, the knower is un-broken and un-remembered, and on the extinction of the delusion-
impression the Lord-ness itself arises — the jīvanmukta, in whom nothing new is established.

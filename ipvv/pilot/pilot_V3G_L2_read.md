**Causality is the conscious knower's agency**

In the previous vimarśa the relation between the known and the knower was shown to be the very
expansion of the action-power. Now Abhinavagupta turns to the other great relation: cause and effect.
And he announces his thesis without hesitation: **the essence of cause and effect is nothing but the
relation of agent and object of action.** Causality, in the end, is the agency of a conscious knower.
Everything that follows — eleven kārikās of argument against the inert, the Buddhist, and the Sāṃkhya
— serves to prove that single claim.

It begins with the Lord. This ancient knower manifests the world of beings without break. How does he
do it? By his will — the un-divided, un-ordered will that is non-different from him. And where are
these beings for him? In his infinite power: all beings are, ultimately, his own powers. This
will-driven manifesting is precisely his action, his making, his creating. The objection — "if the
will is eternal, then creation and destruction would be simultaneous; if occasional, then it needs
another cause" — is answered: the will is tinted by the particular thing to be made, and so it is not
simultaneous with everything at once.

Now the hard work: to show that the *inert* cannot be a cause. Take the seed and the sprout. The
inert seed cannot endow the sprout with being — for the sprout is either already existent (in which
case nothing is acquired) or non-existent (in which case it cannot come to be, since the non-existent
has no nature by which to gain existence). The claim that the effect is both existent and non-existent,
or neither, is self-contradictory. The blue cannot become yellow, however hard we try; so the
non-existent cannot acquire existence. Hence the inert, material cause has no real causal power. The
real cause is elsewhere.

The cause, Abhinavagupta argues across the next kārikās, is the *conscious*. The potter creates the
pot by his power; the yogin, by his will alone, brings about an effect without any clay or seed. If
the inert seed and clay were the true causes, this would be impossible. But it is not impossible —
which shows that the true cause is consciousness, the free will that shapes the effect. The whole
apparatus of the inner and outer, the two appearances, resolves into this: the effect arises from the
conscious knower's will, and ultimately from the Lord.

And what of inference — the way we come to know causes at all? Here Abhinavagupta examines the
own-nature reason (svabhāvahetu). It has two kinds. In the first, the cause-effect relation is clear
and embryonic: "it is fiery because it is smoky," where fire is the cause of smoke. In the second, the
relation is reversed. Take the famous example: the śiṃśapā, a kind of tree. From "this is a śiṃśapā"
we infer "this is a tree." But here there is no *otherness* — the śiṃśapā-ness is not a separate thing
from the tree-ness; it *is* the tree-ness in a specific form. So the entailment holds (wherever the
śiṃśapā-ness is, the tree-ness is), even though the genuine causal relation that requires two distinct
things is absent. This is the basis of all inference: the cause is necessarily present with the
effect, fixed by the determinative power. And the śiṃśapā, born from its own seed by the rule that a
thing arises from its own full complement of causes, is precisely the śiṃśapā-ness that excludes
non-tree-ness.

The upshot: causality, even at the level of inference, is grounded in a real, necessary relation —
not in the power of the inert. The Buddhist's and the Sāṃkhya's accounts of causality, far from
threatening this, actually rest on the same basis when understood rightly. And this entire edifice
shows that the agency of the knower — which is the essence of causality — is not something that could
belong to a limited, non-lordship being. It requires the Lord. Causality, in the end, is the Lord's
will at work: the relation of agent and object of action, expanded through the whole world.

grounds the knower's agency in the Lord.

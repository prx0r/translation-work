**Inference across knowers, and the mirror of consciousness**

We have said that causality is the conscious knower's agency. But this draws a sharp objection from
the Buddhist idealist, the Vijñānavādin. "If everything is appearance," he says, "then cause and
effect are only relations *between appearances*. An un-manifested fire is nothing at all. So how can
the smoke-appearance arise from it? And how can you infer the fire from the smoke?"

Abhinavagupta's answer is subtle and rests on the one creation. The Vijñānavādin, holding each
conscious stream to have its own private appearances, could find cause and effect only within his own
smoke-and-fire appearances — he can never reach another's. But in our view this is wrong. The Lord
creates the appearances *for all the knowers at once, as one*. So when we grasp the pervasion — that
smoke implies fire — the smoke-appearance and the fire-appearance belong, in the Lord's single
creation, to every knower alike. The smoke-appearance arises from the fire-appearance, and this
pervasion can be grasped, once, for all. And for the whole range of knowers — from the worm to the
omniscient — the fire-appearance is one, not split by the difference of streams; so the smoke can
arise from it and from nothing else. That fire-appearance, being indirect to the one who infers, is
what we call the "cause of the Lord" (adhipatipratyaya); it is new, not like the smoke that is born of
smoke. It is the non-wandering, effect-marker, by which the inference is actually made. So inference
holds across knowers, because the Lord has created the appearances as one.

Now, the dependence of cause and effect — the "in-this-existing-this-is" that the pervasion expresses.
Here Abhinavagupta is careful. The dependence is not a real *otherness* between two separate things.
The fire and its heat: their distinction is only constructed. The fire, as the shining own-marker,
cannot have the heat as a separate own-form — both are of equal cause, both come from the one reality.
So in truth the dependence is one, quickened by the synthesis, by the awareness that joins them. The
grammatical point — the locative "in fire [there is] heat" — is not meaningless, nor does it point to
a merely fabricated referent; it is real, and it is supported by the one knower. The dependence of
cause and effect is not two things leaning on each other; it is one thing known as one.

But what if the Sāṃkhya, with its own account of transformation, offers a better story? Here
Abhinavagupta shows that even the Sāṃkhya's "dry" cause-and-effect is not dry. The Sāṃkhya says: there
is one substance — the seed, or the primal principle — with an ordered variety of natures; the earlier
nature is the cause, the later is the effect; the whole evolves by transformation (pariṇāma). But
Abhinavagupta's point is that the transformation itself is an *action*. The transition from the
earlier nature, given up, to the later nature, newly unfolded, is precisely the agent-act-object
structure — the freedom of transforming, which is agency. So even in the Sāṃkhya view, causality is
not the dry change of a thing; it already contains the free knower's act. The intellect, the earth, the
sprout — all are states of the one substance, and the transformation that runs through them is an
action.

And finally, the great closing argument — why only *consciousness* can bear difference and
non-difference at all. The inert, Abhinavagupta says, is a fixed form. It is a known object, and as a
known object it is "different" with respect to another thing (the blue as against the non-blue) and
"non-different" with respect to itself. But the *very same* nature cannot be both different and
non-different — that is a contradiction. So the inert, being a known object, cannot be many-natured.
But consciousness is different. It does not fall into the state of a known object. Like a clear, free
mirror, it bears the whole variety of appearances as its *reflections* (pratibimba) — and those
reflections are distinct, even within the one mirror, without the mirror being torn apart. So
difference-and-non-difference — the very structure of the world, the cause in the effect, the one in
the many — fits only in consciousness, never in the inert.

The objection — "then why don't the elephant and the mountain, appearing in the one, conflict with its
one-ness?" — is answered: because they are *in the mirror*. They do not touch the mirror's own form;
they are its reflections. And the free mirror shows all, even by its mere limpidity alone. This is its
might. The whole vimarśa thus closes on the mirror-image of consciousness: the one reality that can
bear the world's difference-and-non-difference, because it is a free, clear light that reflects all
without being divided by any reflection.

  freedom); the states (intellect/earth/sprout); the triguṇa; the inert as fixed form, both-different-

  the elephant/mountain in the mirror; the mere-limpidity might.

difference-and-non-difference.

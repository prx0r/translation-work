**The will as cause, agent, and action**

The whole vimarśa has argued that causality is the conscious knower's agency. Now comes the sharpest
objection of all, and it threatens to undo everything. If consciousness is *one* — if all the world is
a single consciousness and the difference of things is only ignorance — then how is action possible at
all? For action needs an agent and an object, two distinct things; but if all is one, there is no
distinct object for the agent to act upon.

Abhinavagupta's answer is that action is the one *will-to-do* that joins the agent and the object. The
jar and the consciousness are, by appearance, two. But there is a single re-recollection (parāmarśa)
marked by the will-to-do — the moment "I will make this" — which rests, as it were, in the other
consciousness-essence and binds the two together. It is this one will-recollection, and not any
external contact, that makes the jar an object of action and consciousness its agent. Without it there
is no action, even in a perfectly unified consciousness.

But here Abhinavagupta turns on the very idea that the difference is a mere "ignorance." If
consciousness is one, and the difference is ignorance, then we must ask: *of whom* is this ignorance?
The one-form Brahman cannot have an ignorance that is the mere absence of something else. Nor can the
separate souls exist — they too would be raised up by the ignorance. If the ignorance is called
"un-explainable" (anirvācya), then we still do not know whose it is; "it shines and yet is
un-explainable" is the speech of a yakṣa, a being we cannot pin down. And in fact the difference is
real: our cognition rests in difference as it does not rest in non-difference, and the very "refutation"
that would remove difference is itself a manifestation — and so would be difference, and so would be
ignorance. The whole attempt to dismiss the world as an un-explainable illusion cuts the horns of the
hare's head and plucks the sky-flower. Even if we appeal to the revealed teaching, that teaching too
would be ignorance — a net of illusions. (And indeed, as Abhinavagupta will say, "even Brahman with
ignorance is [admitted by some], so this is not an absolute monism either.")

What, then, of the division of the powers — will in the Lord, knowledge in Sadāśiva, action in Īśvara?
This is not a separation of real things; it is one action-power, seen under different aspects. The
action-power in the action is nothing other than the will. As the teaching says, "the three subtle
powers dwell in harmony." And the grammarians' own analysis confirms it: the action-tattva is seen in
the agent even when the other adjuncts are absent, and is not seen when the agent's complement is
absent — so by presence-and-absence, it is the *resting in the agent* alone that is the action. Even
where no explicit agent appears — "here it does not cook" — the agents fit for cooking and enjoying are
manifest, implied by the locus. Action is not a thing that happens to an inert object; it is the will
at rest in the knower.

And so the final synthesis: the will (icchā) itself is the cause-ness, the agent-ness, and the action.
The three are one act of consciousness. The cause does not cause, the agent does not act, the action
does not move — all three are the one will, by which the Lord, wishing to stand, becomes the world of
jar and cloth and all the appearances. This is the action-power: the will of the one consciousness,
which is at once what causes, what acts, and what is done. With this, the fourth vimarśa of the
kriyādhikāra — the reflexive-awareness of the cause-effect relation — comes to its close.

**Verdict:** understood and readable. The vimarśa closes with a clean, powerful argument: action is
possible in the one consciousness because it is the one will-recollection joining agent and object;
the "un-explainable ignorance" is a reductio; and the will itself is cause, agent, and action — the
whole causal structure is one act of consciousness.

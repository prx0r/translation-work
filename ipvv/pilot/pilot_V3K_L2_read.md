**The coverings and the categories of the known**

Descending from the pure realm, we come to the world of the bound knower. Here the pure self is not
seen as it is; it is covered by a set of limiting sheaths — the *kañcukas*, literally the "armours" or
"coverings." These are niyati (fixity), kāla (time), rāga (attachment), vidyā (the limited knowledge),
and kalā (the limited agency). In the bound state, the one-flavored reality is seen as divided, and
the self's "I-ness" is mistakenly found in what is not the self — in the body, the breath, the mind.

Take the first of these, niyati, the fixative power. In the world we say: the seed is the cause of the
sprout, not of the pot; the clay is the cause of the pot, not of the sprout. This apparent *fixing* of
each effect to its own cause — this is niyati. But Abhinavagupta's point is that this fixity, which
seems to be a feature of the things themselves, in truth belongs to the *knower*, not to the known. In
reality the Lord alone shines forth as "this is the cause of that"; the binding of each cause to its
effect, as it appears, is a covering laid over the reality by the limited knower. So too with time,
and with attachment — the whole flux of the mind, rooted in desire and envy, is the rāga-covering.
These are the armours that hide the pure self in the bound state.

The bound knower — the one whose being is measured by the void-thought, the breath, the body — is the
one who knows the separated objects. He is the māyīya knower, the knower in the realm of māyā, facing
outward toward the distinct things of the world. And the objects he knows are the categories of the
*known* (prameya), which are counted as twenty-three.

They fall into three groups. First, the one *pradhāna* — the root-cause, the un-divided matter out of
which the world evolves (in the Sāṃkhya manner). Second, the *thirteen organs* — the external and
internal instruments of knowing and acting: the five senses, the five organs of action, and the inner
organ with its aspects of mind, ego and intellect. Third, the *ten-fold effect-group* — the five
subtle elements (tanmātras) and the five gross elements, distinguished as subtle and gross. These
twenty-three, Abhinavagupta notes, are the known in the impure realm.

But he adds an important caveat. These categories — the pradhāna, the subtle and gross elements — are
not truly graspable by the common-marker, the ordinary perception of the world. They are not
sensible in the ordinary way; and even their "instrument-ness" is not fitting in the usual sense. So
the categories of the known, in the bound realm, are not things we perceive plainly; they are the
structure of the divided world, laid out by the māyā-power over the one reality.

And so the prathamo vimarśa of the āgamādhikāra comes to its close. It has traced the categories from
the Śivatattva — the body of all — down through the pure group and into the impure realm, where the
self is covered by the kañcukas and the world is divided into the twenty-three categories of the
known. All of it, in the end, is the one consciousness, woven warp and woof, hidden from the bound
knower by the coverings that are themselves, in truth, aspects of the knower rather than of the things.
This sets the stage for the next vimarśa, which will analyze the bound and the Lord (paśu and pati) in
their full distinction.

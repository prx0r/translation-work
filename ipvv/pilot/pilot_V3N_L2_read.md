**The collection of the essence: the one Lord, and how the many arise**

The whole teaching — the self-manifest consciousness, the knowledge-action power, the maheśvara —
has been set out at length through three adhikāras. Now Abhinavagupta gathers it into a brief
collection, the tattvārthasaṅgraha, in fifteen verses, so that the student can hold it in mind and
mediate upon it. One verse states the supreme essence; nine give the bond of the knower and the known;
five give the liberation, which is the recognition. And it all begins with the single great claim.

The own-self alone of all beings is the one Lord, the maheśvara. He is the world-form, and his
re-recollection — "I, this" — is un-broken. This is not a doctrine; it is the discovery of the very
flashing of the self. As the teaching says, all beings are Śiva — the self of all beings is Śiva — and
the self-flashing in each is the Lord. So the first word of the collection is: you are that one.

But then how does the many arise? In the own-self, in the one Lord, by his freedom, a "this-part" is
created, preceded by a contraction. In its midst, the intellect, the breath, the body — known as
"this" — and by the this-ness of that distinct-known, an "I-being" shines, the grasper. He who says
"I am happy," who grasps himself as this contracted being — that is the bound one. And his liberation
is precisely the recognition of what he truly is.

The bound one is "the many person." For truly, nothing is bound: in truth there is only the one free
Śiva, consciousness-natured. But when consciousness contracts and manifests itself as this finite
body and this passing breath, there arises the un-cognition of its own complete form — and with that,
the many persons, each caught in the round of action and enjoyment, which appears to him as pleasure
and as suffering. The one becomes the many; the free becomes the bound; and all through the simple
forgetting of what one is.

The Lord, however, remains whole. In the Lord, everywhere, the manifestation and the reflexive-awareness
are the knowledge and the action; and the māyā-power, which opens up the "this-ness" of things, is
only the covering that lets the world appear. But in the bound one, in the state of self-forgetting,
these very powers appear perverted: the pure manifestation becomes the "being-ness," the rajas of
activity, the darkness of inertia. So the qualities of the world — the sattva, rajas, tamas of the
Sāṃkhya — are nothing but the Lord's own powers, seen in the mirror of the bound soul.

Hence the power-ness is not declared from mere distinction. The bound person, taking himself as one
enjoyer among many, thinks the qualities to be distinct things; but in truth they are the power-form
of the Lord. The being, the bliss, and the action belong to the Lord; in the animal, what appears is
their absence — the two-own mixture of the rajas, the suffering, the attached, the covering.

And yet even here the Lord is present. Even in the mixed and the distinct appearances, in the very
"this-ness" of the world, there is the non-conventional, innate this-ness-recollection of the Lord.
The emptiness itself, the void that the seeker meets, is the Rudra-presided — manifesting as the
negation of the "I." For the animal, however, the meanings of the world are the constructed ones: the
"dear-this," the "enemy-this," the memory and conjecture that spin out a world of separated objects,
built by the inner-self, the contracted "I."

So the collection opens with the whole picture in miniature. The own-self alone of all beings is the
one Lord. The bound one arises by the own-form-un-cognition, the many person in the round of
enjoyment. The Lord's powers appear as the world's qualities in the bound soul. And even the bound
one's world of constructed objects, even the void, is grounded in the Lord's non-conventional
this-ness. All the categories, all the knowers, rest in the one free Lord — and this is the foundation
on which the recognition of liberation will be built.

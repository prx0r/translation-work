**The bond and the liberation: how the bound creates, and how he is freed**

We come now to the heart of the matter: the essence of the knower, the known, the bond, and the
liberation. The great question is how the bound knower relates to the Lord. For the bound one does not
merely suffer the world; he *creates* it, after his own fashion. But his creation is not the Lord's.

The bound one's creation is *un-common* (asādhāraṇī) — it is private, his own, per-knower and
per-moment, vanishing with him. Yet it does not stand on its own: it is *sustained by the Lord's
creation* (īśasṛṣṭyujīvinī), riding on the back of the reality that the Lord alone makes. The objection
arises: "if the paśu creates, is his creation not common, like the Lord's?" No. The paśu's own power is
hidden from him — he is other-dependent — and only the Lord-ness can awaken it. The whole difference of
bound and liberated hangs on a single fact: the bound one does not *know* his own power. He forgets
that the world he builds is built with the Lord's own materials. The māyā-power, the word-mass of the
Lord, the wheel of the syllable-deities, the eight-fold class — all this moves through him like the
rising and falling of the breath, and makes the "friend-this, enemy-this" constructions of his little
world. Even his wildest creations — the ten-tusked elephant of his fancy — are only rearrangements of
appearances the Lord has made.

The construction-action, then, is the bond. It is of the breath-form, ever-moving, and it exists
precisely to obstruct the bound one's rest. By it the māyā-power binds the animal. Even the seemingly
free constructions — "I, the king, stand," "I have slain my enemy" — are, on inspection, other-
dependent; the bound one's freedom is only apparent, resting on the Lord's. And this is the māyā in its
threefold working: the appearance of the void, the appearance of the opposite, the mere negation — all
spreading through the three stains.

Against this stands the Lord's own creation. It is *common* (sādhāraṇā) and clear-manifestation —
never constructed, always whole. And there is a path out of the bound one's prison: the abandonment of
construction. When, by the thinning of the constructions and the gathering of the mind to one point,
the bound one comes to the "I-this" of the Lord, his animal-ness is destroyed and the Lord-ness shines.
Even the animal's own creation, if it is known in the light of the Lord's power, becomes common and
real. The whole practice — the one-pointedness, the cultivation — is the letting-go of the constructions
that bind.

And this is the recognition. The world-self — the very one who is the grasper and the grasped, the
manifestation and its mass — that one is the "I." The Lord is not another; he is the "aham" of every
"aham." When the constructions fall into the great re-recollection, they cease to construct; they
become the non-construction, the pure recognition. The bound one sees that he was never bound — that
all along he was the Lord, playing at finitude as a king plays at being a soldier.

What, then, of the known? The known — the objects of the world — is common: the same for the bound and
the liberated. But they know it differently. The liberated one knows it *with the non-difference of his
own self*: he knows the whole knower-group, from the pure realm down to the embryo, as "I-this," one
flavor, un-divided. The bound one knows it in the reversal, as utterly other, divided into things.
The known is the same; the knowing is not.

But in the supreme Śiva — the highest — there is no separate known at all. In the pure realm there is
a known-order; in the while-living-liberated there is; but in the parama-Śiva, the "I"-experience is
filled and nothing stands over against it. It is the consciousness-bliss-mass, the supreme, beyond all
syllables. There is no object there, because there is no otherness there.

And the fruit of the whole teaching is this: knowing one's own self as the Lord-form, seeing the
knowledge and the action as his powers, one knows and does whatever one wishes. Such a one is the
while-living-liberated, the jīvanmukta, who at the fall of the body is simply the supreme Lord. Even
the world of māyā is, in truth, the manifestation of consciousness, untouched by place and time; even
here the Lord is present. The liberation, then, is not the acquisition of something new — it is the
recognition of what one always was. The bond is the forgetting; the liberation is the recognition.

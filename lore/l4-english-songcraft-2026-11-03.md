# L4 songcraft — English renderings of Sanskrit verse, with technicals

*2026-11-03. Grounding lore for the L4 layer (L3 set to music). Every exhibit is
a real published rendering with source. The point: steal the technology, not the
style — each device below names exactly what the translator did to the Sanskrit
and what it cost.*

## 0. The one structural fact

Sanskrit classical meter is **quantitative** (laghu/guru patterns, every syllable
regulated); English meter is **accentual-stress** (stressed/unstressed). Direct
imitation is near-impossible for strict meters and unnecessary for song. Ingalls
(Harvard Advocate essay on translating Sanskrit poetry): *"English meter is
accentual. Sanskrit meter, like that of Greek and Latin, is quantitative...
even Milton would have had to make large compromises in translating Sanskrit"*
(and Milton's Horace Ode imitation is his proof that genius-level compromise
is the ceiling, not the floor). Consequence for L4: **we do not imitate
Sanskrit meters. We set L3 to English music** — rhythm, cadence, rhyme,
refrain — with traces locked (subset rule).

## 1. Sublime exhibits (steal these moves)

### 1a. Ryder, Meghaduta stanza 99/2.41 — rhyme carrying parallelism
Sanskrit builds the verse as interlinked repeating pairs
(aṅgenāṅgaṃ tanu ca tanunā … "body with body, thin with thin, pain with
pain, tears with tears, longing with longing, sighs with sighs").
Ryder (ABABB five-line stanza, rhyme where Sanskrit has none):
> With body worn as thine, with pain as deep,
> With tears and ceaseless longings answering thine,
> With sighs more burning than the sighs that keep
> Thy lips ascorch — doomed far from thee to pine,
> He too doth weave the fancies that thy soul entwine.
*Move:* the Sanskrit's repetition-engine becomes English rhyme-engine
(deep/keep, pine/entwine). *Cost:* the complete-review verdict — "barely
Kalidasa's poem," sanitized (no body-joining: "weave the fancies" where
Edgerton has bluntly "with his body thy body he enters"). Lesson for L4:
rhyme is structuring music, but **rhyme never twists meaning** (rule 17) —
Ryder's "pine/entwine" is exactly the failure mode the subset rule blocks.

### 1b. Ingalls, Kumarasambhava 8.42 — the sunset miniature
> The sun, his horses with bent necks,
> with plumes striking on their eyes,
> goes home, yoke riding high upon their manes,
> setting the day to rest in ocean.
*Move:* miniature brush-strokes fitted "like gems into a neat grammatical
frame" — one sentence, subject ("The sun") delayed-completed ("goes home"),
details nested inside. *Cost:* none — this is unrhymed, faithful, and
beautiful. Ingalls' own gloss: vowel harmony crowns it (each line ends on a
high diphthong until the last sinks with deep "au" into ocean). Lesson for
L4: **vowel music is free** — assonance and line-final vowel weight cost no
doctrine and buy half the song.

### 1c. Ingalls, Kumarasambhava 8.72 — moon-drops, and the double translation
First the song: *"You could pick up the drops of moonlight shaken off by the
leaves and scattered like flowers on the ground beneath the branches, and deck
your hair with them."* Then, to show the Sanskrit's diamond-tight syntax, a
second translation "so literal that it is almost unintelligible." Lesson for
L4: **our stack already owns this** — L2 is the literal diamond, L4 the song.
Never compress both jobs into one layer.

### 1d. Bhavakadevi's breasts — Ingalls vs Paz (same verse, two musics)
Ingalls: *"Her breasts are brother kings, equal in nobility, reared together
till they have reached the same altitude of fame; and from their border
warfare these monarchs of vast provinces have gained a cursed hardness."*
Paz: *"Her breasts are two brother kings, equal in nobility, looking out from
the same heights, side by side, sovereigns of the vast provinces they have won
in frontier battles, with a defiant hardness."*
*Move to steal:* the political metaphor is the verse — both keep every term
(kings, nobility, heights, provinces, battles, hardness); the music differs
in cadence (Ingalls periodic, Paz paratactic). Lesson: **fidelity first,
music in the joints** — word order and sentence rhythm are the cheapest,
safest instruments.

### 1e. Gitagovinda refrain — Miller vs Holcombe (the refrain problem)
Sanskrit song form: dhruvapada (refrain) repeated after every couplet —
"the stable unit of sound and meaning," syntactically resolving the couplets'
dangling compounds. Miller (free verse, refrain as two lines): *"When
spring's mood is rich, Hari roams here / To dance with young women, friend —
/ A cruel time for deserted lovers."* Holcombe (octosyllabic line for the
dance-energy + assonance for yamaka): *"Look to Hari in the spring, /
dancing with his youthful women: / how endlessly the pain encircles / one
who's solitary, my friend."*
Lesson for L4: **the refrain is repeated verbatim, never reworded** — it is
the song's anchor. Octosyllabic lines carry dance-energy; pentameter carries
narrative (Holcombe's rule: octosyllabic for songs, pentameter elsewhere).

### 1f. Brough's English sardulavikridita — the only published meter imitation
Sanskrit (19 syllables/line): *raktāśoka kṛśodari kva nu gatā
tyaktvānuraktaṃ janaṃ …* Brough's English approximation to the same
shape:
> Flame-flower crimson Ashóka-tree, where has she gone?
> Why left she this heart aflame? ...
> Bee-swarms, swarming aloft, alust, cluster in clouds,
> Crave nectar, yet fail to swarm:
Brough's verdict: *"Only on very rare occasions can we hope to be able to
imitate at all closely in translation any of the Sanskrit metres."* His one
clean success is drutavilambita (Vikramorvasiya): *"Ever for you is my love,
ever constant, kind. / Never unfaithful: a heart ever true I gave…"* —
which works because the meter is short and regular enough for stress to
stand in for quantity. Lesson: **imitate only short regular meters, if ever;
default to song-forms, not scanned forms.**

### 1g. Ryder's Shakuntala — stage-song English
*"She is God's vision, of pure thought / Composed in His creative mind"* —
rhymed couplets recreating sloka pulse for performance (staged Berkeley
1907, Greek Theatre, live animals). Lesson: L4 lines should be **speakable/
singable** — the stage test is real: read aloud; if the actor can't breathe
it, rescored it.

## 2. Meter technicals (granular)

- **Anustubh/sloka** (epics, Gita): 8 syllables x 4; only positions 5 (light),
  6 (heavy), 7 (alternating) constrained — the loosest classical meter, closest
  to English ballad/fourteener energy. L4 default for sloka-source verses:
  ballad quatrain or hymn-common-meter (8.6.8.6), doctrine untouched.
- **Mandakranta** (Meghaduta: 17 syllables, GGGGLLLLLG / GLGGLGx, yatis after
  4, 10): "slow-stepping." Pokharel: English imitation would need *"four
  stressed syllables followed by five unstressed in every line"* — possible to
  scan, "well-nigh impossible" to also mean. **Never imitate; render as slow
  processional lines** (long lines, heavy caesuras, end-stopped).
- **Upajati / vasantatilaka / malini / sikharini** (kavya workhorses): long
  lines with fixed cadence-tails. English analogue: lines with **matching
  cadence-tails** (same final stress contour 3-4 lines running) rather than
  full scansion.
- **Arya / matra meters** (mora-counted): closest English kin is syllabic
  verse + loose accentual — count syllables, let stress fall free.
- **Yamaka** (sound-echo, e.g. Gitagovinda): render as **alliteration/
  assonance**, never as rhyme — rhyme adds closure the Sanskrit doesn't have.
- **Compounds (samasa)**: Sanskrit packs a clause into one word; English must
  unpack across the line. Technology: **enjambment as unpacking** — let the
  compound spill over the line-break (Ingalls' "soft under-the-branches-
  fallen-flowers" shows the seam; L4 smooths it: noun first, modifiers
  trailing down the next line).
- **Inflectional binders** (sa ... uma vise-grip, Horace-style interlock):
  English can't inflect-bind; substitute **chiasmus and parallel placement**
  (word 1/word 3, word 2/word 4 across the line).
- **Word order**: Sanskrit puts the verb/sum last ("form like a well-cut
  diamond ... the impersonal verb demands completion by the infinitive and
  object which come only at the end"). English song can **keep the
  end-weight**: delay the resolving word to line-end for the same gathered
  release — without changing syntax roles.
- **One Sanskrit line → ? English lines**: Holcombe's rule of thumb —
  one line by two English lines when content demands it; octosyllabic for
  songs, pentameter for narrative; musical aim, no forced rhyme. Ryder's
  Meghaduta used 5 lines ABABB for 4 (rhyme-imposed expansion — flagged as
  cost). Recommendation: **default 2 English lines per Sanskrit line-pair
  (couplet), 4 per full verse; expand only for refrain.**

## 3. Poetic-license ledger (what translators actually do — allowed in L4?)

| License | Example | L4 verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Rhyme where Sanskrit has none | Ryder ABABB | Allowed ONLY if every rhyming word traces to L3 ids (subset rule decides) |
| Line-count change (4→5, 1→2) | Ryder, Holcombe | Allowed; form recorded in `form` |
| Refrain repetition | Gitagovinda dhruvapada | Required verbatim, never reworded |
| Compound unpacked across lines | Ingalls moon-drops | Core technique; enjambment |
| Vowel music / alliteration / assonance | Ingalls au-sunset; Holcombe yamaka | Free — costs nothing |
| End-weight / delayed resolution | Ingalls diamond | Free — English syntax kept |
| Epithet selection among equals | "gasping" vs "thirsty" (ghatakarpara) | Allowed with rasa reason in notes |
| Sanitizing (dropping body-union) | Ryder stanza 99 | FORBIDDEN — subset rule + rule 6 (never smooth) |
| New metaphor for rhyme's sake | "heaven's crown" | FORBIDDEN — subset rule rejects |
| Archaic diction ("doth", "thee") | Ryder | Avoid — contemporary singable diction |

## 4. L4 working defaults (proposed)

1. Couplet default; quatrain for long meters; refrain-form for song texts.
2. Octosyllabic lines for dance/song passages, pentameter-ish for
   sastra/didactic, long processional lines for mandakranta-source.
3. Rhyme sparing (couplets, refrain-anchors); assonance/alliteration everywhere.
4. End-weight doctrine words line-final.
5. Refrains verbatim-repeated.
6. `form` recorded every verse; first verse per work needs owner verdict.
7. Read aloud = gate zero. If it won't scan, it won't parse.

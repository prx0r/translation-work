# Tantrāloka 23 — Abhiṣekaprakāśana: the consecration that makes a teacher

*Assessor's scholarly briefing, 2026-11-06. Written for whoever (human or model)
has to decode this ahnika. Not canon; the Sanskrit is canon. Sources consulted:
Dyczkowski's TĀ translation (vol. 9, ch. 16–27); Dupuche, The Kula Ritual
(ch. 29 appendix and the consecration discussion); Kāmika and
Siddhayogīśvarīmata as cited inside the text; Jayaratha's Viveka for joints;
Gnoli (Italian, bare text) as a cross-check; Sanderson on the Śaiva canon;
Mālinīvijayottara as the underlying authority for consecration.*

## What this ahnika is doing, in one breath

Aḥnika 22 ended with the removal of the initiate's old marks (liṅgoddhāra) —
the Vaiṣṇava converted, the outsider admitted. Aḥnika 23 now asks the question
that ritual cannot dodge: *who is allowed to make other people?* Initiation
(dīkṣā) gives a person fitness to be taught and to worship. Consecration
(abhiṣeka) gives a person the *authority to confer* that fitness. They are not
the same rite and not the same power, and the whole ahnika is built on the
distinction. This is the chapter on the making of a guru.

With a24 (funerary rites) waiting immediately after, the arc is stark: a23
makes the teacher, a24 buries him (or the one who died before teaching).
Abhinavagupta places the conferral of teaching authority inside the same
initiatory complex that handles death — the tradition is being given a
succession, not merely a ritual.

## The argument, in the order the ahnika makes it

**1. Fitness first (v1–10ish).** The ahnika opens by naming the rite: the
*procedure of consecration of the Supreme Lord* (pārameśvara abhiṣeka) is now
declared — and immediately notes that the putraka-dīkṣā already described is
"yuktimat", i.e. it brings one into connection with the categories that are to
be enjoyed. The Sanskrit wants us to hear that initiation has already made the
candidate *capable*; what is missing is *authority*.

Then the gatekeeping. Abhinavagupta cites the **Kāmika** (a Siddhānta temple
authority, not a Trika text): a guru who does not know the *adhva-sandhāna* —
the joining of the six paths — is not a guru even if he has been consecrated
through the whole samayī succession. He cites the **Siddhayogīśvarīmata** for
the same point from another angle. This is characteristic of a23: Abhinavagupta
is not inventing authority, he is *assembling* it from the wider Śaiva canon
and making the Trika the place where those authorities are fulfilled. When the
text cites Kāmika in a chapter about making teachers, the citation is itself an
argument: the Trika guru stands inside the Siddhānta's own standards, and
exceeds them.

**2. Who may be examined, and when (the famous snag).** The verses on women,
non-men, śūdras and others who desire the goal make a distinction that has been
read badly by moderns in both directions. The text says, in substance: *do not
scrutinize them at initiation; investigate at the time of knowledge.* That is
not a liberal admission and not a conservative exclusion — it is a statement
about **which rite does the sorting**. Dīkṣā is not the place for the fitness
exam; the teaching moment is. Do not modernize this into a rights claim, and do
not read it as blunt exclusion. Report the rule the text makes, in the order it
makes it, and flag the interpretive question rather than resolving it for the
reader.

**3. What a consecrated guru does (v11–30ish).** Once consecrated, the guru must
*mutter and meditate the whole mantra-paddhati* — the line of mantras — and
then act: initiation, exposition, and the rest, for fit disciples, never for
the unfit. Abhinavagupta then lays out the **sapta-satraka**, the seven
"sacrifices" the consecrated one is bound to protect: initiation, exposition,
compassion, friendliness, and the giving of food and the rest. The list is
crucial to the tone of a23: the authority conferred is not a licence, it is a
*duty-set*. The guru is authorized for a group of four tasks (manifestation and
the rest) and, the text says with unusual directness, is there to *lend a hand
to those who are falling*, not to push them down.

**4. The consecration itself (v~20 to ~90).** The rite, in the sequence the
text gives:
- the guru, having worshipped and offered as before, **announces his intention**
  (cikīrṣita) to the disciple;
- he **sprinkles him sixty-four times** (catuḥṣaṣṭyā abhiṣiñcet), then once
  more with water charged with the essence of the mantras (tad-mantra-rasa-toya);
- the rite is done "with might and greatness", extensively (vibhavena,
  suvistīrṇam);
- he then tells the disciple what has happened to him;
- **the former guru is released** (tyakta-adhikāraka): from that point he acts
  as he wishes regarding teaching and initiation, unbound, because *a lineage is
  like a lamp kindled from a lamp* (dīpa-dīpavat). If he does not pass it on
  before he dies, the line is annihilated.

That lamp image is the heart of the chapter. Authority here is not a substance
that gets divided when it is given away; it is a flame that is transmitted
without loss to the giver. The guru who has given it is *free* precisely
because he has given it. Read vv. in this cluster against the dīpa-dīpa line and
they stop looking like ritual mechanics and start looking like a theory of
succession.

**5. The close (v~100–103).** The ahnika ends by distinguishing what is *not*
being conferred here: not the assignment of adhikāra in the sense of the
*upāya*-fitness already given in dīkṣā, not the vow of knowledge
(vidyā-vrata); what is conferred is the *procedure of consecration* — and the
last verse seals it by citing **Parameśvara** as the one who declared this rite.
The citation at the end mirrors the citations at the start: the chapter is
bookended by appeals to the wider canon, and the Trika teaching is presented as
the place where those appeals land.

## The lore you need in your head while reading this

- **Putraka vs ācārya.** Initiation makes a *son* (putraka) — one fit to be
  taught, to worship, to receive. Consecration makes a *teacher* (ācārya) — one
  fit to make sons. The fourfold putraka scheme (sabīja/nirbīja, niṣkala, etc.)
  belongs to the wider initiatory complex; a23 assumes it.
- **Sixty-four.** The number is not decorative. In the Kula material (Dupuche,
  ch. 29) sixty-four lamps are set out for the master's consecration,
  worshipped as the sixty-four kula goddesses, with Kuleśvara and Kuleśvarī in
  the centre, and the consecration is completed by the **hand of Śiva**
  (śiva-hasta) — the disciple's hand, charged with mantra, that moves of itself
  and strikes the body at the point that reveals his kula. The sixty-four
  sprinklings of a23 and the sixty-four lamps of a29 are the same cosmological
  number: the yoginī families, eight times the eight mothers. If you are
  glossing a23 and the number sixty-four appears, this is the resonance to
  hear — but keep a23's *public* rite distinct from a29's *secret* one.
- **Adhva-sandhāna.** The "joining of the paths" is the technical competence the
  Kāmika demands: the guru must be able to map the six paths (varṇa, mantra,
  pada; kalā, tattva, bhuvana) onto one another and onto the disciple. A guru
  who cannot do this is a ritual technician, not a guru. This is why a08–a12
  (the paths) precede a14–a23 (initiation): you cannot consecrate a teacher who
  cannot join the paths.
- **The lamp.** dīpa-dīpavat is the image to hold. Compare the Buddhist and
  Śaiva uses of the same image elsewhere: transmission without loss, and the
  freedom of the one who has transmitted.
- **Siddhānta vs Trika.** Kāmika is Siddhānta (dualist, temple-centred, Tamil
  country, 28 principal āgamas). The Siddhayogīśvarīmata and Mālinīvijayottara
  are the Trika/Kaula side. Abhinavagupta cites both. He is not ecumenical for
  its own sake: he is showing that the Trika guru satisfies the strictest
  Siddhānta criteria *and* the Kaula ones, and that the Trika is their unity.
  Every time you see a citation in a23, ask what work that citation is doing.

## Where the traps are

- **Abhiṣeka ≠ dīkṣā.** Do not let a gloss collapse them. dīkṣā = fitness
  conferred on the disciple; abhiṣeka = authority conferred on the teacher.
  Sanskrit near-synonyms (saṃskāra, adhikāra) must be glossed with the
  distinction held.
- **adhikāra has at least three senses here**: fitness for a means (upāya), the
  right to perform a rite, and the authority to confer it. Follow the local
  sense; flag the shift when the text slides between them.
- **The women/śūdra verse.** Report the rule; mark the interpretive question.
  No smoothing, no modernizing, no editorializing.
- **Sixty-four.** Do not import a29's sexual-kula content into a23's public
  consecration. The number is shared; the rite is not.
- **Print/text.** The a23 text has known uncertainties around the mantra-names
  and the sequence of the sprinkling; Jayaratha's Viveka is where the variants
  are adjudicated. Where the Sanskrit is damaged or the sequence ambiguous, say
  so in the L0 grounds — never invent a clean reading.
- **The release of the guru** (tyakta-adhikāraka) is easy to mistranslate as
  "abandoning his authority" in a negative sense. It is the opposite: he is
  released *because* the transmission is complete. Translate the freedom, not
  the loss.

## How to hold the ahnika as a whole

A23 is a chapter about *how authority moves*, written by someone who had
authority and was careful about how it is passed. It answers the question a22
left open (the outsider has been admitted — admitted to what, and by whom?) and
sets up a24 (what happens when the teacher dies before or after transmitting).
Read it as: gatekeeping (who may be examined, and when) → duty (what the
consecrated one owes) → rite (the sixty-four sprinklings) → theory of
succession (the lamp) → delimitation (what this rite is not). That is the arc;
everything else is ornament or citation. And the citations are not ornament:
they are the argument that the Trika stands where the whole Śaiva canon was
pointing.

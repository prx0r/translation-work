Tradition: Non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir

Emerging from the literature of two prominent Śākta traditions that flourished in Kashmir, the Trika and the Krama, a body of texts developed that distanced themselves from the particular ritual and mantra systems of their scriptural background while translating and articulating a non-dual view of selfhood, cosmology, and corresponding practices. This "translation" of Śākta scriptural traditions, particularly in their Kaula phase, involved a more outward-facing and generalized Śaiva teaching, even while theologically and phenomenologically elaborating the Goddess-centered doctrines and principles of the Trika and the Krama. An early text to take this step toward a broader Śaiva non-dual vision was the Śivasūtra, revealed to Vasugupta. The Spandakārikā, likely composed by his disciple Kallaṭa Bhaṭṭa, is another early non-dual Śaiva text of this kind, and both garnered a significant number of commentaries, which expanded this literature from its less elaborate sūtra- and kārikā-based beginnings. These texts were less focused on cosmology and metaphysics as such, and they dispense with references to mantras and rituals of initiation. Instead, they initiate a significant process of internalization of tantric ritual and yoga, streamlining them into more direct awareness practices (although the Śivasūtra preserves some elements of classical tantric yoga in its third chapter).

The next major phase of non-dual Śaivism in Kashmir comes with the development of a lineage of texts that are progressively more philosophical: the Śivadṛṣṭi of Somānanda, and the extraordinary corpus of his disciple Utpaladeva, followed by the commentaries of Abhinavagupta. While Somānanda initiates this philosophical turn and outlines some of its major contours, Utpaladeva's Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā fully establishes this non-dual Śaiva philosophy as a śāstra, whose ideas become foundational to later tantric exegesis within and beyond Kashmir. This corpus of texts is also involved in a process of translating the views and cosmologies of Śākta scriptural traditions, but does so for an expanded audience by utilizing the discourse of Sanskrit dialectics, epistemology, and pan-Indian methods of debate.

Beginning in the seventeenth century, a collection of Sanskrit texts emerges from a lineage of Śrīvidyā initiates who made significant contributions to the earlier tradition of non-dual Śaivism that flourished in Kashmir, while creatively drawing from many other early modern trends in Śākta, Vedānta, and Bhakti traditions. The most prolific and creative author of this lineage is Sāhib Kaula, whose writings have been studied, translated, and contextualized by Jürgen Hanneder. These texts include stotras (e.g., Śārikāstotra, Śivajīvadaśaka, Sahajārcanaṣaṣṭikā), paddhatis (Śrīvidyānityapūjāpaddhati, Śyāmāpaddhati, Hṛlekhāpaddhati), and the Devīnāmavilāsa, a literarily complex reworking of the Bhavānīsahasranāmastotra, whose third chapter paraphrases the entire Pratyabhijñāhṛdaya of Kṣemarāja. Many of these works include ritual and devotional worship of Northeastern Goddesses (Dakṣiṇakālī, Bhuvaneśvarī, Tārā), which the Kaula lineage may have been responsible for first introducing into the region of Kashmir. Sāhib Kaula's most unique work, unprecedented in Sanskrit literature, is the Kalpavṛkṣa, which is the subject of a monograph by Jürgen Hanneder. This work is difficult to categorize, since it visually maps multiple textual sources and integrates them into a single text that includes citations moving in multiple directions across a lattice-like structure (similar to the European carmina cancellatum) and incorporates "intexts" from thirty languages. The overarching purpose that Sāhib Kaula assigns to this work is to demonstrate how "Advaita Vedānta, Śaiva non-dualism, and supreme non-dualism are all the final conclusion of all philosophical systems." This de-emphasis on doctrinal difference, in order to allow for a harmonization of Advaita Vedānta with forms of non-duality that previously flourished in the Kashmir Valley, is part of a larger transregional trend. Two examples are the complex rapprochement taking place between Śrīvidyā and Vedānta during this period and the integration of Śākta concepts and practices with general non-dual Vedānta teachings implicit in much of the Haṭhayoga literature associated with the Nāthas, which James Mallinson refers to as a "fortuitous union of non-dualities."

In addition to Sāhib Kaula's corpus, there is a body of stotras and ritual paddhatis composed by the next generation of this lineage—Jyotiṣprakāśa, Cidrūpa Kaula, Gaṇeśabhaṭṭāraka, and Sadānanda Kaula—as well as by Jyotiṣprakāśa's son, Govinda Kaula. These works include a corpus of gurustotras and hymns to Tripurasundarī and Bhairava. Finally, the writings of Bhāskarakaṇṭha, a contemporary author, can also be included in this tradition, particularly his sub-commentary (Bhāskarī) on Abhinavagupta's Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī, the Mokṣopāyaṭīkā, and the Cittānubodhaśāstra. Another important author of the eighteenth century is Śivopādhyāya, who composed the Vijñānabhairavavivṛti.

Tradition: Late Śaiva Siddhānta

The traditions referred to as Late Śaiva Siddhānta are largely a product of the Tamil country. Beginning in the fourteenth century, we see, on the one hand, the "rediscovery" of a large corpus of Śaivāgamas bearing the names of works in the classical canon that are largely concerned with festivals and temple worship. Many of these sources show an unprecedented orientation toward Tamil literary traditions, such as the veneration of the Nāyaṉārs, and toward regional sacred geographies, including the incorporation of Murukaṉ and other previously neglected deities. These texts often discuss subcategories of Śaiva practitioners, such as the Ādiśaivas, specific to the region, or even presume them to be the intended audience. In response to these scriptural sources, Śaivas in Tamil Nadu produced a vast, mostly unstudied literature on both ritual and social practice.

From the twelfth century onward, we also see the emergence of Tamil-language Śaiva scriptures, such as Meykaṇṭatēvar's Civañāṉapōtam and sub-commentaries like the Civañāṉasitthīyār, which provide a new conceptual and philosophical vocabulary for thinking about Śaiva traditions that represents a direct challenge to the dualistic, upper-caste-centric reading of the Āgamas presented by Sadyojyotis and Aghoraśiva. This new Tamil Śaiva idiom proved so successful that even figures like Śivāgrayogin, who were more sympathetic to the classical exegetical tradition, largely accommodated their early modern writings to this framework.

Tradition: Śrīvidyā

The youngest of the classical traditions of Śākta Tantra, the veneration of the goddess Lalitā or Tripurāsundarī and her iconic Śrīyantra is the most prevalent tantric tradition in the world today. Emphasizing the aesthetic beauty of the supreme goddess, as opposed to her more ferocious aspects aligned with the culture of the cremation ground, Śrīvidyā introduces a new way of thinking about the organization of tantras—in terms of sections on pūjā, mantra, and cakra—and a new and deeply influential approach to the encoding of mantra. This subdivides the baseline fifteen-syllable (pañcadaśī) main mantra into three subcategories of kūṭa mantras (Vāgbhava, Kāmarāja, and Śakti). As they assume increasingly subtler forms, these core mantras can then be converted into higher mantra expressions such as the Kāmakalā, which are then increasingly consciously mapped onto the production and sustaining of higher states of consciousness. The different branches of Śrīvidyā are then in turn subdivided according to whether the initial kūṭa mantra begins with the letter "KA," "HA," or "SA," with the so-called Kādi transmission representing the dominant approach. After higher levels of initiation, following a Pūrṇadīkṣā / Pūrṇābhiṣeka—such as the Śāmbhavadīkṣās and the Saparyās—the use of the Pañcadaśī mantra is replaced by Mahāṣoḍaśī, and the form of the Śrīyantra and its number of enclosures (āvaraṇa) become increasingly elaborate.

What we call "Śrīvidyā" emerges out of the coming together of a range of originally distinctive traditions. These include a cult of the god Kāmadeva—associated with love magic—as presented in the Nityākaula and Vāmakeśvarīmata; the Pratyabhijñā philosophy of the Non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir, integrated into the Yoginīhṛdaya and then the commentarial tradition beginning with Śivānanda; and the veneration of a cycle of Nityā goddesses associated with the phases of the moon as taught in the Tantrarājatantra. Both the theology and the practice of most forms of Śrīvidyā are also heavily indebted to the Trika, especially the Ekavīrā cult of the goddess Parā, and to the Kubjikā tradition, whose Saṃvārtamaṇḍala contributes to the shape of the Śrīyantra itself. More fundamentally, Śrīvidyā provides the common ritual language, the mode for presenting mantras, and the styles of textual presentation that most early modern Śākta traditions take as their model. Through works such as the Saubhāgyaratnākara of Vidyānandanātha, it has also redefined how ritual is taught within the genre of the paddhati.

In the early modern period and into the contemporary period, Śrīvidyā has thrived as the primary face of purity-conscious esoteric ritual associated with the Śaṅkarācārya orders and their Smārta disciples. It is also out of this context that the stotras dedicated to the tantric goddess Tripurā, such as the Saundaryalaharī and the Lalitāsahasranāma, have become part of wider popular culture, a unique development among the classical Śākta tantras. As reflected in works like the Śrīvidyārṇava, Śrīvidyā has also come to operate as the central medium through which more archaic forms of classical Śākta tantra are preserved in the form of higher-level initiations transmitted within Śrīvidyā adept networks. Less scholastic forms of Śrīvidyā are also integral to the majority of non-elite and non-brāhmaṇa forms of Śākta worship rooted in textual resources, such as is found among the Nātha yogīs. Finally, in the form of the reformed Kaulism of Bhāskararāya and his disciples, Śrīvidyā has been presented as offering a new reconciliation of Kaula tantra with Advaita Vedānta.

Tradition: Smārta

Just as the term "Śrauta" is used for those who study and revere the "Śruti," especially by performing the traditional Vedic sacrifices in which Soma is produced and distributed, a "Smārta" is, technically speaking, someone who specializes in the various supplementary traditions of "Smṛti." The term Smṛti refers to the array of textual traditions that support the performance and understanding of Vedic ritual. Traditionally, this body of tradition would have included the Śrauta- and Gṛhyasūtras, the Vedāṅgas (such as grammar and astrology), the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata, the Purāṇas, Dharmaśāstra, as well as Sanskrit literature and philosophy. In practice, however, "Smārta" has come to refer to a collection of distinctive, primarily Brahmin forms of identity usually aligned with a specific tradition of Vedāntic study and monastic lineage. Outside of South India, Smārta traditions also tend to be identified with the custodianship and implementation of Dharmaśāstra as a discipline.

In the context of the MIRI collection, texts fall under the Smārta tradition when they are composed by authors from within those traditions, especially when they take as one of their major objectives reconciling Tantric knowledge with Smārta concepts and norms. Some key models for accomplishing this task include reframing Tantric Knowledge as representing an "upāsanākāṇḍa" that complements the karmakāṇḍa and jñānakāṇḍa of Pūrva- and Uttaramīmāṃsā. Another common feature is the reordering of saṃpradāya-specific ritual identities within the framework of a Pañcāyatana model, where the worship of Gaṇapati, Sūrya, Viṣṇu, Devī, and Śiva are seen as forming complementary parts of a ritual whole. Another frequent approach systematically homologizes the process, the language of expression, and the goal of Tantric worship with bodies of knowledge transmitted by traditions of Advaita Vedānta aligned with Śaṅkarācārya.

In terms of the root tantras themselves, two foundational works are the Śāradātilaka Tantra and its commentary by the Maharashtrian Rāghavabhaṭṭa, and the Kerala-composed Prapañcasāra in the lineage of Śaṅkarācārya. The MIRI library also contains a large number of works by the South Indian Tamil Advaita theologian Upaniṣadbrahmendra, based for much of his career in Kanchipuram. These comment upon the so-called "Yoga-Upaniṣads," texts that incorporate a large amount of material about subtle-body yogic practices that they share with Śākta tantras in general and with the writings of Nātha Yogīs in particular. In light of the pivotal role played by Smārta traditions in the early modern period, and their considerable influence on Tantric textuality, MIRI aims to incorporate in the future key works from the canon of Smārta traditions.

Tradition: Śaiva Siddhānta

As a scriptural corpus, the Classical Śaiva Siddhānta canon comprises eighteen Rudrabheda and ten Śivabheda Āgamas, along with a series of supplementary Āgamas (such as the Mataṅgapārameśvara). Of these classical texts, the earliest of which seem already to be in evidence in the fifth century CE, only the Niśvāsa, the Pauṣkarapārameśvara, portions of the Svayambhūva and the Raurava, the Kīraṇa, the Parākhya, and perhaps elements of the Vātula survive.

Classical Śaiva Āgamas are addressed to a range of audiences that could include the baseline initiate (samayin), the seeker (sādhaka), the spiritual heir to the guru (putraka), as well as the preceptor himself (ācārya), and they present an array of practices specific to each of these constituencies. Each text teaches that ritual initiation (dīkṣā) provides both social and soteriological empowerment, while also committing the new initiate to a program of daily ritual and a code of observances (samaya). The texts cover a range of concerns, from preparing the sacred ground to constructing the maṇḍala, procedures for both external and visualized modes of daily (nitya) and occasional (naimittika) worship, fire rituals and modes of expiation (prāyaścitta), as well as several categories of special observances (vrata, caryā). Many texts also contain discussions of cosmology and present a range of approaches to yogic visualization founded on simple postures and breath control. Prior to the twelfth century, Classical Śaiva Āgamas express no interest in large-scale public worship, ritual on behalf of a paying client, or discussions of festivals. Outside the Pārameśvara tradition, the earliest scriptures do not even discuss the making or use of the śivaliṅga.

When read on their own, these internally diverse Śiva-centric ritual traditions seem to express a variety of views on the nature of reality and the relationship between Śiva and the individual jīva. Beginning in the seventh century, however, the theologian Sadyojyotis commented upon the Āgamas from a strictly dualistic perspective. It is his interpretation and mode of apologetics, supported by Bengali authors such as Brahmaśambhu, Kashmiri Śaiva dualists such as Nārāyaṇakaṇṭha and Bhaṭṭa Rāmakaṇṭha, and Tamil exegetes such as Aghoraśiva, that became the dominant face of the tradition.

Unexpectedly, the primary systems of ritual presented in the ritual manuals of the classical Śaiva Siddhānta, such as the eleventh-century Somaśambhupaddhati of Somaśambhu and the Kriyākramadyotikā of Aghoraśiva, are not actually based on any of the root texts of the Rudra- and Śivabheda corpora. In fact, they are almost exclusively derived from two auxiliary scriptural sources, namely the Kālottara, an auxiliary work in the tradition of the Vātula, and, in the case of Aghoraśiva's Mṛgendrapaddhati, the Mṛgendra.

Tradition: Śākta General

Over the past several thousand years, the veneration of the Great Goddess has also taken many forms. In this tradition we include types of Śākta traditions that do not associate themselves with the classical Śākta Āgamas but also do not affiliate with the early modern East Indian goddess traditions, such as the worship of Tārā, Dakṣiṇakālī, or the Mahāvidyās. We also include here works that are primarily focused on alchemy, defensive and practical magic, and the types of transactional tantric practice that for many today represent the most familiar face of "Mantra-Tantra." The occasional worship of Durgā and various forms of Purāṇic goddess worship are also treated as representing the general (sāmānya) form of Śākta tradition.

Tradition: Kaula General

Kaulism is perhaps best understood as a style of worship as much as it is a separate tradition, and as such Kaula modes for venerating a range of deities have been integrated into many, if not most, of the tantras in the MIRI archive. The Kaula traditions seem to originate in the veneration of generic gods and goddesses of the Cakra (Cakreśvara / Cakreśvarī), paired with the Eight Mother Goddesses, with worship using transgressive substances (originally just three, as opposed to the Pañcamākaras) conducted in a style derived from the Kāpālikas. Conceptually, these early Kaula traditions tend to emphasize the centrality of the human body as a primary site for ritual and ecstatic experience, even to the extent of treating spiritual experience (anubhava) as a veridical authority (pramāṇa). The tradition that descends from the Kulasāra, one of the earliest of these works, teaches a theology of radical non-dualism, distinct from Advaita Vedānta, that it calls Paramādvaita. The same work is also the first to explicitly present the role of the Jīvanmukta, who "liberated, causes others to be liberated." Both frameworks would prove pivotal to the non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir.

Kaula textuality can be divided into several phases. The first major distinction is between early texts that speak generically about embodied incarnation and those works (for example, the Kaulajñānanirṇaya or the Akulavīra tantras) that feature the siddha Matsyendranātha and his disciples as primary revealers of Kaula traditions in the current age. In a later phase of these discourses, the Kulārṇavatantra, with its equation of Kaulism and the authority of the Upaniṣads, emerges as a central locus of scriptural authority. The tradition also includes a number of early modern works that frame their Kaulism around conceptual categories drawn from East Indian tantric traditions without focusing on the preferred deities of those lineages.

Tradition: Tantranibandha

The genre of the nibandha first emerged in the twelfth century within the discipline of Dharmaśāstra. Whereas earlier scholars had tended either to organize their arguments around the structure of the text they were commenting upon or to produce guides to practice privileging one particular dimension of a specific tradition, the nibandha gathers enormous compilations of citations drawn from a range of sources and traditions and then organizes them to make an argument. Central to this approach is the assumption that all of the texts upon which these resources are drawn worship in a parallel manner, share core values, and are effectively part of the same tradition, a position that often does not align with what is taught in the texts themselves.

The Tantras seem to be among the first traditions to adopt the genre of the nibandha, and when they do so, the nature of Tantra as a knowledge system begins to change. Instead of each tantric tradition being defined by its distinctive theology, cosmology, and narrative traditions—many of which were mildly, or more so, in opposition to each other—Tantras increasingly become encyclopedic collections of different mantra applications addressed to a range of often unrelated deities from across the boundaries of Śaiva, Śākta, and Vaiṣṇava lineages.

Tradition: Kālīkulakrama

The Kālīkula comprises a huge canon of Kālī-based rituals and systems focused on wrathful Goddesses, whose points of origin are distinct from those of the Trika. One form of the Krama that emerges from this milieu is a Śākta system focused on a set of thirteen Kālīs (twelve surrounding a central Kālī) or seventeen Kālīs, who are identical in appearance. Originally connected to the rays of the sun and twelve calendrical phases, or to the phases of the moon, these Kālīs later come to represent different phases of the process of perception, thus comprising a dynamic maṇḍala. The earliest articulation of the Krama is attested across scattered passages within a massive compendium of early Śākta literature, the Jayadrathayāmala, which includes myriad systems of ritual, antinomian observances, and occult practice centered on a large array of Goddesses and forms of Bhairava, some of which may be indebted to Vaiṣṇava Pāñcarātra sources.

Two early, exclusively Krama scriptural sources, preserved in Nepalese manuscripts, are the Devīpañcaśataka and the Kramasadbhāva. Both situate their revelation in the Karavīra cremation ground at Uḍḍiyāna, and their colophons report that they were revealed by a certain Śrīnātha, which may be an alias of Jñānanetra, an important early Siddha of the Krama tradition. Later Krama texts from Kashmir identify Jñānanetra as the agent of revelation (avatāraka) of the Krama esoteric teaching, and report his reception of the Krama directly from the Goddess Maṅgalā or Vīrasiṃhā Svāminī (likely names for Kālī, the leader of the Pīṭheśvarīs) in the charnel ground of Uḍḍiyāna. This teaching/awakening was then transmitted through successive generations, which include many authors who envision their own awakening as a reenactment of Jñānanetra's illuminating encounter with the Goddess, a unique feature of the Krama's notion of transmission. Jñānanetra's only surviving text, the Kālikāstotra, also initiates a longstanding tradition of devotional hymns or songs, which become one of the prized media of transmission and composition in the tradition. A more comprehensive system of Krama liturgy, predominantly internal and with numerous phases, is articulated in a triad of later works that all share the same name, the Mahānayaprakāśa. These texts all trace their lineage back to Jñānanetra.

There is also a set of texts that include short, pithy instructions (kathā, carcā, gīti, sūtra, chummā), which can be placed under the rubric of awakening through oral teachings or songs (kathana). These include the Kaulasūtras, the Chummāsaṅketaprakāśa, and the Vātūlanāthasūtra. The first two texts are connected to teachings received by and transmitted through another important Siddha of the Krama lineage, Niṣkriyānanda, traditionally identified as Durvāsas (Krodharāja), whose earliest mention is in the Uttaragharāmnāya of the Ciñciṇīmatasārasamuccaya. This Kubjikā scripture includes two short Krama tracts, the Kālikākramaślokadvādaśikā and the Kālikākramapañcāśikā, and also includes a brief frame story detailing how the ascetic Vidyānanda received these teachings from Niṣkriyānanda in the mountains in the northern part of Śrīśaila, another important revelatory site for the branch of the early Krama that teaches a cycle of Kālīs.

In fact, it was in South India that the Kālīkula Kālīkrama would assume its most recognizable form, the Mahānaya, which would then subsequently be reimported into both Kashmir and Nepal. This tradition originally develops across a series of works all of which are entitled Mahānayaprakāśa. At least two of these, the Mahānayaprakāśa of Arṇasiṃha/Vīrasiṃha and a somewhat later anonymous work found in Kerala that directly reincorporates elements from Abhinavagupta's teachings in the Tantrāloka, were produced in South India. Though they offer distinct teachings and otherwise claim separate transmitters, both Mahānayaprakāśas claim to represent a tradition originating with Hṛsva-nātha's disciple, the Kāpālika Cakrabhānu. Regardless, within them we see the reorganization of Krama liturgy unknown to Abhinavagupta, where, as we see in Arṇasiṃha's Mahānayaprakāśa, an older mode of a fourfold reality becomes conjoined with a fixed but elaborate pantheon, which is said to manifest as the Assembly of the Five Currents (pañcavāha), the Assemblies of Light (prakāśacakra), the Assembly of Bliss (ānandacakra), the Assembly of the Form of Emptiness (khamūrticakra), and the Assembly of the Multitude (vṛndacakra), comprising sixty-four Siddhas and the goddess Maṅgalā. It is this version of the tradition that is known to the Śivānanda who writes a major commentary on the root text of Śrīvidyā, his disciple Śrīvatsa, who composes the Cidagaganacandrikā, and their emerging circle of disciples and associates, mostly situated in the Tamil country. This is also the lineage of Maheśvarānanda, based at Cidambaram, whose masterful Mahārthamañjarī synthesizes Krama ritual with one of the most elaborate and insightful re-thinkings of the teachings of the Non-dual Śaivas of Kashmir, building upon Utpaladeva's core insights in a newly ecumenical manner, namely that the "Supreme I-consciousness" is individuated as one's particular personal self. It is from within this lineage also that the familiar understanding of Śiva Naṭarāja as constantly performing the five acts escapes from the narrow confines of learned esoteric exegesis to become part of a broader popular South Indian mode of understanding.

It is the Krama as it was known to Maheśvarānanda and his circle—which has adopted the five flows and incorporated linguistic and conceptual borrowings from South Indian Dravidian languages—that is adopted by Vimalaprabodha and transmitted into the Kathmandu Valley, where it becomes an integral part of the living lineages of the Sarvāmnāya. This is what Vimalaprabodha primarily teaches in his Kālīkulakramārcana, composed at the court of Arimalla, though it is important to recognize that Vimalaprabodha also inherited other distinctive Krama transmissions closely related to the later Nepalese traditions of Guhyakālī worship, in which the goddess is depicted with an array of animal heads and stands upon a jackal. As Maciej Karasinski has recently demonstrated, though ascribe their traditions to an explicitly Kashmiri Krama lineage descending from a figure perhaps called Prabhūtānandanātha, it is also Maheśvarānanda's and Vimalaprabodha's tradition of a five-flow Mahārtha that forms the foundation of the living tradition of Kālīkrama preserved in Kerala among the Mussad tradition of Śākteya Brahmins.

Tradition: Kubjikā

The most extensive surviving corpus of classical Śākta tantra belongs to the "crooked" goddess Kubjikā. Many of these texts were composed in the western Deccan, in what is now southern Maharashtra and Goa. Deeply indebted to the scriptures of the Trika, the worship of Kubjikā incorporates the "Ancient" tradition's mantra system into its own distinctive visualization of the octagon-shaped Saṃvartāmaṇḍala, supplementing it with its own nyāsas and the iconic "pañcaprāṇava" mantras.

One particularly distinctive feature of the Kubjikā tantras is their rich narrative traditions. The texts repeatedly recount how the goddess assumed her iconic "crooked" form, while also relating her changing relationship with Bhairava over many ages. For the first time in the tantric traditions, we are also presented with numerous stories about the descent of particular siddhas into the human world. Such tales would have a profound influence upon the emergence of the Nātha yogis, who also inherit from the later Kubjikā tantras the distillation of earlier, more diversified representations of the yogic subtle body into the six-plus-one cakra system with Kuṇḍalinī at its center—no longer treated as an extension of ritual liturgy—that is most familiar to us today.

Tradition: Nepalese Ṣaḍāmnāya and Sarvāmnāya

In the eleventh century, within works such as the Ciñciṇīmatasārasamuccaya, the various transmissions of the Classical Śākta tantras—especially the Trika, the Krama, and the Kubjikā traditions—begin to develop a new way of thinking about their relationship with each other. They emerge not as rivals, but as complementary. Nonetheless, they remain distinct parts of a dynamic and unified Classical Śākta tradition, organized in terms of three or more streams corresponding to the cardinal directions. When this emergent model of scriptural currents is transferred into the Kathmandu Valley (Bhaktapur, Patan, and Kathmandu) under the Malla kings, it largely displaces other rival approaches to thinking about the tantras and gradually becomes the dominant model for understanding and preserving the Classical Śākta traditions. Instrumental to these developments is the thirteenth-century South Indian Śākta yogī Vimalaprabodha, who brings to the court of King Arimalla both his vast library of tantric works and his considerable genius as a ritualist. Indeed, a close reading of his Paścimārcanapaddhati, which despite the title is in fact dedicated to Śambhu, the deity of the Śāmbhavāmnāya, reveals him already to be teaching his own idiosyncratic fusion of Kālīkrama, Śāmbhava, and Kubjikā traditions, within which Lalitā Tripurasundarī plays a subordinate role.

In the realm of scripture, in works such as the Parātantra, for example, a more explicit model of three or four Āmnāyas is then expanded and refined to incorporate an even wider range of traditions. Thus, the Parātantra presents the Adhāmnāya as incorporating the pantheons of Vajrayāna Buddhism, and also places a new emphasis on the significance of the previously more marginal tradition of Śrīvidyā. Some of the Parātantra's solutions, such as preserving a distinct Dakṣiṇāmnāya for the Bhairava tradition while treating Śrīvidyā as the Ūrdhvāmnāya, would eventually be substituted for treating Śrīvidyā itself as the Dakṣiṇa stream while inserting the increasingly popular Kaula tradition of the Kulārṇava and sometimes also the Śāmbhava at the apex. Nevertheless, the general structure of this Sarvāmnāya—"All the Traditions"—approach, with the Trika as the Pūrva, the Krama as the Uttara, the Kubjikā as the Paścima, and Śrīvidyā as the Dakṣiṇa, becomes profoundly important for understanding the Tantric traditions of the Kathmandu Valley. The Sarvāmnāya also re-enters India, where it becomes one of the primary frameworks through which the rest of Classical Śākta tantra gets reintegrated into high-level esoteric Śrīvidyā practice at elite levels of initiation. Additionally, the Sarvāmnāya framework preserves a number of other distinctive Śākta traditions, especially the cult of Siddhalakṣmī, whose tantras have otherwise vanished from the subcontinent. In this tradition we will also include ritual manuals and texts specifically associated with the sacred geography of the Kathmandu Valley.

Tradition: Northeast Indian Tantra

This tradition incorporates a distinct corpus of early modern works of Śākta tantra, ritual manuals, and commentaries that are composed in Mithila, Assam, and Bengal, as well as works that derive the structure of their pantheons from such tantras. The reason the tradition is treated separately is that, with few exceptions, these works represent a conscious break from the structures and pantheons that define the classical Śākta tantras and offer an even more fundamental erasure of the idiom and influence of the Non-dual Śākta tantras of Kashmir. Thus, for example, the Kālīs of the Krama and their scriptural models are replaced by a different sequence of sixteen Kālī-Nityā goddesses, or perhaps a new centrality is given to Dakṣiṇākālī standing upon the body of Śiva.

We also see a new focus on figures such as Tārā and Ugratārā in her many manifestations, or Nīlasarasvatī. In place of the Āmnāya system, emerging out of Mithila around the sixteenth century (they are known, for example, to Āgamavāgīśa in his Bṛhattantrasāra) and then spreading across the subcontinent, a canon of Ten Mahāvidyā goddesses (often Kālī, Tārā, Tripurasundarī, Bhuvaneśvarī, Bhairavī, Chinnamastā, Dhūmavatī, Bagalāmukhī, Mataṅgī, and Kamalā) becomes increasingly popular. These deities are then treated both as a set of resources for applied mantra-prayogas and as the core pantheon whose mantras must be acquired to gain access to higher levels of practice. In the more Kaula sources, frameworks such as dividing modes of practice into those for Paśus, Vīras, and Divyas, each requiring increasing degrees of ritual eligibility and posing greater potential risks, become ubiquitous. In some works affiliated with Tārā, there is the emergence of a new ritual modality, called Mahācīnācāra, said to have been imported from the East. In many cases, there is also an explicit accommodation of Kaula transgressions within a ritual setting to the social standards of an emerging Kulin Brahmanism.

Tradition: Bhairava Tantras

In the Classical Tantras, the term "Dakṣiṇa" refers to an eightfold canon of scriptures that take as their supreme deity various forms of the terrifying lord, Bhairava. Though elements drawn from this collection of traditions are preserved in other sources, particularly the Buddhist Kriyātantras and the Jayadrathayāmala, among the foundational texts of these traditions only those concerned with the self-willed lord (Svacchanda Bhairava) and the Lord of Nectar (Amṛtīśa Bhairava) have come down to us intact. The Svacchanda Tantra is particularly important, for alone among the early sources it takes as a key focus the teaching of a theology of absolute freedom (svātantrya), a position that is reflected in the social practices of its adherents. This tantra also provides us with some of the most detailed accounts of Tantric cosmology and the mechanics of forms of yoga oriented toward observing and manipulating the passage of time and the contemplation of the inner voids. Both this cosmology and the textual traditions of tantric yoga seem to be inheritances of the Atimārga tradition. In a similar manner, the Netra Tantra of Amṛtīśa Bhairava transmits a series of teachings on apotropaic practices used to defend both a king and a yogī from hostile forces, as well as a threefold series of Śākta Yogas that are closely connected to earlier traditions on cataloguing different types of malevolent beings, the conditions that may render human beings susceptible to their influence, and the corresponding remedies. Though neither text, strictly speaking, adopts such a position, both of these texts were commented on from a non-dual Śaiva perspective by the eleventh-century Kashmiri exegete Kṣemarāja.

Tradition: Yoga

While many tantras integrated the teaching of a six-component (ṣaḍaṅga) form of yoga into their systems of practice as part of the performance of both daily ritual and occasional observances, it is only around the eleventh century that we begin to see the emergence of a genre of texts that take yoga as their exclusive focus. Many of these texts place a new emphasis on postural practices and the body as the primary locus of practice, forming the foundation for what comes to be called Haṭhayoga, while separating the practice of yoga from liturgy or a specific ritual calendar. There is also a deeper exploration of practices of breath control (prāṇāyāma) and retention (kumbhaka). While our earliest source, the eleventh-century Amṛtasiddhi, belongs to the tradition of Vajrayāna Buddhism, this type of literature was rapidly taken up by a range of communities, from the Nātha yogis to Vaiṣṇava ascetics, from the Vīraśaivas to the Smārtas.

Tradition: Pāñcarātra

Contained within the Mahābhārata, the Nārāyaṇīya teaches that the divine reality comprises a processual formation (vyūha) that, taken as a whole, is Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa himself. Each facet of the divine person (Nārāyaṇa, Vāsudeva, Saṃkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna, and Aniruddha) corresponds to a succession of states within each human being (thinking, feeling, willing, knowing, acting) and also to the process whereby a person loses track of, and then recovers, an awareness of their true nature. The Vaiṣṇava Āgamas of the Pañcarātra descend from this tradition, and they preserve some of its key features, such as the story of the journey to White Island (śvetadvīpa) and the recurring incarnation down through the ages of Nara and Nārāyaṇa, for example in the form of Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa. Even the earliest Pañcarātra Āgamas to which we have access, such as the Svayambhuvapāñcarātra, the Vāsudevakalpa, and the Devāmṛtapañcarātra, have some parallels in the Niśvāsa corpus, but represent an earlier form of the Pañcarātra tradition than what is found in the Jayākhyasaṃhitā, which is more dependent on Śaiva and Śākta systems. Some later works in the tradition, such as the Lakṣmītantra and the Ahirbudhnyasaṃhitā, have even been directly influenced by the writings of the Non-dual Śaiva exegetes of Kashmir, such as Kṣemarāja.

The Pañcarātra Āgamas are organized according to a canon of "three jewels," the Jayākhya, the Sātvata, and the Pauṣkara. Each work is focused on a different pantheon. For example, while Narasiṃha appears only once in the Jayākhyasaṃhitā, the man-lion plays a pivotal role in the Sātvata, especially in the chapters on initiation. As both a source of ritual and theology, the Pañcarātra plays a foundational role for the tradition we call Śrīvaiṣṇavism, with two of the most important ācāryas of the tradition, Yāmuna and Vedāntadeśika, writing śāstric defenses of the legitimacy of the tradition, along with a ritual manual ascribed to Rāmānuja himself. Much as with later South Indian Śaivism, the early modern period sees the wide circulation of a large corpus of Pañcarātra scriptures focused on the construction and management of temples and the conducting of festivals, many of which are dialogical with the Tamil tradition of the Āḻvārs.

Tradition: Śaiva General

Over the past several thousand years, the veneration of Śiva has taken many forms. In this tradition we include types of Śaivism that do not associate themselves with the classical Śaiva Āgamas. This may be because they predate them entirely, as in the case of the Pāśupatas. Or, as with the Śrauta Śaivism of Haradattācārya, they may belong to traditions that do not emphasize their authority.

These texts may also reflect lay traditions of worship and storytelling or present modes of worship primarily associated with pilgrimage to particular tīrthas. We also include in this tradition examples of a more generalized mode of Śaivism that does not express allegiance to the theology and practice of any particular tradition.

Tradition: Pratiṣṭhā

Apart from a short section in Varāhamihira's Bṛhatsaṃhitā and several chapters in the Matsyapurāṇa, the oldest surviving discussions of the making of iconic and aniconic foci for worship, urban design and infrastructure, the design of temples, and the awakening and installation of images in a ritual setting are contained within the tantras. Unlike later works on Vāstu, texts such as the Devyāmata of the Niśvāsa, the Piṅgalāmata, the Mayasaṃgraha, the Bṛhatkālottara, and the Mohacūḍoṭṭara are frequently addressed not only to the ācārya himself, but also to the various categories of artisans whom he both trains and initiates. Key portions of these texts present the reader with modes of Śaiva and Śākta pedagogy and theology as they were presented to audiences not trained in classical Indian philosophy. Not only do these texts contain some of our most detailed accounts of pre-modern material culture, such as procedures for the making of liṅgas with and without faces, as well as mūrtis in a variety of media, they also present some of the earliest and complete accounts of festival culture and non-purāṇic theorization of sacred landscapes.

Tradition: Vedānta

As part of the next stage of its development, the MIRI library intends to become the primary digital resource for studying, in all of its rich variety, one of India's defining philosophical traditions, the discipline of Vedānta, which investigates the relationship between Brahman, reality, and the individual human soul.

In the broadest sense of the term, "Vedānta" (literally, the end of the Veda) refers to works such as the Bṛhadāraṇyaka of the Śukla Yajur Veda, the Katha of the Kaṭhaka recension of the Kṛṣṇa Yajur Veda, or the Chāndogya of the Tāṇḍya branch of the Sāmaveda. In their original context, each of these texts formed the concluding portion of a particular Vedic śikhā, offering narratives, dialogues, and philosophical speculation that complement the liturgies, ritual procedures, and prose exegesis that make up the vast majority of the Vedic textual tradition. In the ancient world, a student would have been introduced to the specific work associated with their hereditary tradition as the culmination of their studies. Only the rare exceptional scholar would then have tried to master the Vedic transmissions of other lineages. It is only when these texts came to be separated from specific śikhā transmissions, disconnected from the theory and practice of specific Śrauta rituals, and were 'read together' that they came to be viewed as part of an ever-extending, self-contained canon of works in a similar style labelled Upaniṣads (a term that originally referred to only some of the specific esoteric teachings inside these works). By extension, the term Vedānta is used to refer to any discipline that is inspired by and engages with this Upaniṣadic canon.

As a scholastic category, however, Vedānta is not simply a label that gets applied to any text that responds to the imagery and values contained within the Upaniṣads. As a technical term, Vedānta invokes a shared model of textual exegesis. As a śāstra, Vedānta deploys interpretive reading strategies from the Pūrvamīmāṃsā school of Vedic exegesis as a tool for making sense of the intended meaning of the Brahmasūtras of Bādarāyaṇa. Much like the Mīmāṃsā sūtras of Jaimini, the Brahmasūtras elevate select statements scattered across the many Upaniṣads, which they represent as the essence of the tradition. Bādarāyaṇa then provides an analytical framework for reading all of these texts together as posing and answering a set of questions expressing a single comprehensive argument about the nature of reality. It refutes the positions of rival philosophical schools and provides a method for ordering and then enacting a range of beneficial spiritual practices inspired by statements in the Upaniṣads. Through writing texts such as the Śārīrakamīmāṃsābhāṣya, the Śrībhāṣya, and the Brahmasūtrabhāṣyānuvyākhyāna, ācāryas such as Śaṅkarācāryabhagavatpāda, Rāmānujācārya, and Madhvācārya set out to establish that the philosophy of the Brahmasūtras was aligned with their respective positions (whether non-dual, qualified non-dual, pluralist, or dualist) about the relationship between Brahman, reality, and the individual human soul. While each of these saṃpradāyas would also produce comprehensive commentaries on individual Upaniṣadic texts, the śāstric traditions of these competing versions of Vedānta primarily focused on 1) further explicating key arguments in the Brahmasūtra commentaries, 2) refining the use of Mīmāṃsā hermeneutical models, and 3) producing philosophical polemics and apologetics aimed at refuting rival schools. Especially from the thirteenth century onward, these activities became increasingly central to the textual cultures of Indian philosophy and the practice of Indian religions, to such an extent that by the eighteenth century, especially in much of North India, the legitimacy of a sampradāya came to be practically defined by its possession of its own commentarial tradition on the Brahmasūtras, and the figure of the wandering yogī fused with that of the sannyāsin who had internalized the message of the Upaniṣads. These scholarly traditions find their counterpart in the equally influential and much less well-recognized flourishing of textual traditions of lay Vedāntic instruction and devotion, expressed in both Sanskrit and the vernaculars, as well as models for yogic practice centering the repetition and contemplation of oṃ as guided by the tradition of the Pañcīkaraṇa, originally ascribed to Śaṅkarācārya, but which would ultimately influence a range of Vedāntas.

Building on the existing strengths of its collection, the MIRI library will initially seek to incorporate aspects of the Vedāntic traditions, especially Advaita Vedānta, that are directly engaged with the tantric traditions through citing their texts, integrating their conceptual frameworks, dialoguing with their authors, or incorporating their practices. Such an approach will already include key texts ascribed to foundational figures such as Śaṅkarācāryabhagavatpāda, Rāmānujācārya, and Madhvācārya, along with major intellectuals such as Vidyāraṇya, Appaya Dīkṣita, and Upaniṣadbrahmendra. In this manner, the MIRI has begun to establish itself as a hopefully indispensable resource for paṇḍits and scholars of Indian philosophy whose interests do not extend directly to the world of the tantras.

Tradition: Mantranaya/Vajrayāna

The classical traditions of the path of mantras (Mantramārga) are defined by a common approach to the use of mantras, a style of textual composition, and a collection of shared practices that influenced a range of religious traditions both within and beyond the subcontinent. Though today we are most familiar with "Tibetan" or Vajrayāna Buddhism as it is practiced in Tibet, Nepal, and the greater Himalayan region, both the tantric scriptural traditions that form the foundation of what is often called the way of mantras (Mantranaya), as well as their traditions of exegesis and ritual, are rooted in a vast literature that had its origins throughout the Indian subcontinent. The MIRI archive thus contains works of Buddhist scriptural revelation, such as the Guhyasamājatantra, that share both core concerns and even textual parallels with the Śaiva and Śākta tantras. It also incorporates compositions by Buddhist scholar-practitioners, such as the eleventh-century Mahāsiddha Advayavajra, that synthesize these teachings in the service of instructing disciples. By attending to both the commonalities that these sources share with their Śākta-Śaiva "cousins," as well as their distinctive features, we enrich our understanding of the moments of dialogue and exchange that were essential features of the early medieval Indian environments in which many of these traditions often flourished side by side.

Tradition: Vaiṣṇava General

The early modern period (c. 1400–1800) sees the emergence of at least two canons of Tantric literature that became deeply influential among the Vaiṣṇava bhakti traditions of North and North-East India. One such stream of revelation venerates Kṛṣṇa and his dalliances with the gopīs of Vṛndāvana, which are recast in the language of tantric ritual and visualization. These texts became extremely important for the followers of Caitanya of the Gauḍīya saṃpradāya and are cited repeatedly, for example, in the Haribhaktivilāsa of Caitanya's disciple Sanātana Gosvāmī.

The other, even less well-studied tradition, exemplified in Muktabodha's collection by the Agastyasaṃhitā, is dedicated to imagining Rāma and Sītā as the divine couple whose exploits are to be ritually imitated. This elaborate system of worship demands an entirely new system of mantras and liturgy, resulting in the production of a substantial unpublished paddhati literature, such as the Rāmārcanacandrikā of Ānandavana. Though foundational to the tradition of the Rāmarasikas, Rāma-centric tantras have never been the focus of sustained academic inquiry, and it is hoped that the MIRI Digital Library will make possible a deeper understanding of this neglected religious community.

Tradition: Trika

Perhaps the oldest surviving form of Tantric Śāktism, the original name of this transmission is the "Pūrva," or the Ancient Tradition. Though it has largely been eclipsed by other forms of Classical Śākta Tantra, the Trika's reputation today is largely founded upon the fact that it provides much of the scriptural foundation for the Non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir. One of its most important scriptures, the Mālinīvijayottara, is the explicit commentarial focus of Abhinavagupta's exegetical ritual manual, the Tantrāloka, which presents itself as a guide to both the theology and practice of the Pūrva as a system. Following his guru Śambhunātha, Abhinavagupta is strongly influenced by a series of late Trika scriptures, such as the Trikahṛdaya and the Vīrāvalī, that taught an explicitly non-dual version of the tradition in which external ritual practice is substantially replaced by mental visualizations. In these texts, the Ancient Tradition's original triad of the goddesses Parā, Parāparā, and Aparā, along with the goddesses Mālinī and Māṭṛkasadbhāva—each embodying the letters of the Sanskrit language presented in diverging sequences—were complemented by the integration of the worship of the Kālī of the Krama.

Except in citations from the Kashmiri exegetes, however, this highly esoteric form of the Trika has not been preserved. Nor do we have direct access to the once equally influential part of the tradition, also frequently cited in the Tantrāloka, dedicated to Triśirobhairava. What survives today are several early Trika tantras that either primarily present the early ritual system, such as the Siddhayogeśvarīmata and the Tantrasadbhāva, or, like the Mālinīvijayottara and the Vijñānabhairava, are mostly concerned with yogic visualization practices. What these texts share is a distinctive theology that emphasizes the capacity of mantras and language both to release and to bind human beings, and the power of the human imagination as a means of achieving liberation from transmigration.

Another important work, which received a commentary (now lost) by Somānanda and one by Abhinavagupta (vivaraṇa), is the Parātrīśikā, which focuses exclusively upon the Goddess Parā, particularly her sonic nature as the root mantra SAUḤ. This Trika scripture became foundational for a corpus of Trika works composed in South India beginning in the twelfth century, and is sometimes distinguished by the appellation Parākrama or Ekavīrā. Texts in this tradition include the corpus of Madhurāja, who was from Madurai, Tamil Nadu, and his son Kṛṣṇadāsa's Parātrīśikālaghuvṛttivimarśinī. There is also the Parātrīśikātātparyadīpikā composed in Cidambara, the Anuttaraprakāśapañcāśikā of Ādyanātha, the Parāprāveśikā (alias Svarūpaprakāśikā) of Nāgānanda with a commentary (vimarśinī) by Cidānanda, an anonymous Anuttarasaṃvidarcanācarcā, the Parārcanakrama included in the Aghoraśivācāryapaddhati, the Parākramavāsanā of Śāmbhavānanda, the Parāpaddhati in the Nityotsava of Umānanda, and the Anuttarapaddhati of the Paraśurāmakalpasūtra. The fact that some of these Trika materials are housed in Śrīvidyā works invites future research on the relationship between these traditions during this period.

Tradition: Śāmbhava

The family of traditions variously labelled Śāmbhava, Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava, Śāmbhavī vidyā, and so forth, are perhaps the least well understood of the living tantric lineages that claim a textual foundation rooted in classical sources. While this state of affairs is a product of the very preliminary nature of our research, it also reflects a complex history of multiple receptions across a range of communities. From the perspective of most contemporary Śrīvidyā practitioners, the Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava is simply a specialized mode of esoteric practice, much of it associated with the ritual veneration of the Gurupāduka mantras, reserved for elite initiates. In a similar manner, for Kaulas following either in the tradition of the Kulārṇavatantra or working within the Nepalese Āmnāya system, under the guise of the Ūrdhvāmnāya, late stage initiations reveal the presence of the hidden face of the god Śambhu. Often identified with the non-dual teachings of the Upaniṣads, Śambhu presides over the totality of the Śākta systems. Moreover, in the vernacular traditions of Telugu, Kannada, and Marathi-speaking wandering yogīs and householder practitioners, the tradition of Śambhu or Śāmbhavī vidyā is perceived not as a supplementary mode of tantric ritual, but rather, as a yogic transmission comprising a range of meditative practices, often associated with the use of an internal or external consort. Finally, when it has been noticed at all, western scholars have equated the Śāmbhava with the Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava, presenting the practice as a lesser known South Indian tradition. Such scholarship asserts that the Śāmbhavāmnāya is simply a specialized expression of the Paścimāmnāya. In it, the goddess Kubjikā has been decentered in favor of her male consort Naveśvara or Navātman Bhairava. Over time, it is assumed, this minor tradition was mostly absorbed into Śrīvidyā.

As we begin to explore the Śāmbhava as a textual tradition based on a growing collection of unpublished sources, a more complicated picture has begun to emerge. Core elements of the tradition seem to originate outside the realm of the āmnāya transmissions of Śākta tantra. What early works such as the Śambhunirṇaya (which does not label itself Kaula or incorporate the basic Kaula goddesses) and the Matsyendrasaṃhitā (a Kaula work which privileges this pantheon) share is a common concern with the transmission of an archaic body of yogic knowledge only distantly related to what is taught in classical tantra. From the perspective of these early sources, what we perceive as the heritage of the āmnāya system is treated as a supplementary ritual modality. Unexpectedly, although present from the very beginning, proto-Śrīvidyā in particular plays a very minor role in these early traditions, a feature that persists in some of the earliest paddhatis. The transformation of the Śāmbhava into the Ṣaḍanvayaśāmbhava appears to occur at the level of commentary, gradually emerging almost contemporaneously in South India and the Himalayas within traditions of exegesis and ritual systemization. In South India, this takes place within the lineage of the South Indian Śivānandanātha, while in Nepal, it occurs under the guidance of Vimalaprabodha. In both cases, teachers who were primarily remembered as Śākta initiates deemphasized the extra-ritualistic yogic dimensions of the Śāmbhava practice and assimilated it more comprehensively into the Āmnāya traditions. Further research drawing upon key works such as the Śāktakramanirṇaya of Umākānta should help us to gain a better understanding of the broader history of these developments, which seem to be closely related to the practice of Āmnāyasamaṣṭi in early modern Śākta tantra along with the origins of the Ṣaḍāmnāya/Sarvāmnāya.
